Baker-Gordon Syndrome

Overview

Baker-Gordon syndrome, as currently molecularly defined in synaptotagmin-1 (SYT1)-associated reports, is an ultra-rare neurodevelopmental disorder usually caused by heterozygous de novo SYT1 variants or disruption. It presents from infancy with hypotonia and global developmental delay, often with severe communication impairment, movement disorder, visual dysfunction, behavioral dysregulation, sleep disturbance, and frequent electroencephalography (EEG) abnormalities; seizures are not obligatory.

  • Epidemiology: Ultra-rare; no population prevalence estimate; recognized SYT1 cohorts include 11 and 40 individuals, age range 0.2–34.3 years.
  • Etiology: Predominantly de novo dominant SYT1 missense variants at conserved C2-domain hotspots, especially p.Ile368Thr; rarer intragenic deletions and structural/regulatory disruptions.
  • Manifestations: Infantile hypotonia, global developmental delay, severe speech/language impairment, adaptive and autistic features, self-injury, sleep disturbance, strabismus/nystagmus/cerebral visual impairment, feeding/respiratory problems, and hyperkinetic movement disorders; seizures 4/22 in one cohort, while EEG abnormalities are common.
  • Prognosis: Heterogeneous; some delayed gains and later ambulation, others severe/profound lifelong dependence; movement disorders, sleep difficulty, self-injury, and adaptive decline may increase with age; respiratory/feeding-related deaths reported.
  • Diagnostics: Suspect with early hypotonia/developmental delay plus communication or motor impairment, visual signs, movement disorder, behavioral features, or abnormal EEG; confirm by trio exome/genome sequencing with parental testing, plus SYT1 dosage/structural testing when indicated.
  • Differential Diagnosis: Angelman-like/Rett-like syndromes, developmental and epileptic encephalopathies and seizure mimics, dyskinetic cerebral palsy, monogenic movement disorders, Coffin-Siris/KBG/Sotos-like dysmorphism, metabolic/mitochondrial/acquired causes, and NALCN/UNC80 disorders sometimes labeled Baker-Gordon.
  • Management: Supportive and multidisciplinary: early habilitation, communication/educational supports, swallow/nutrition and respiratory/sleep care, ophthalmology and orthopedic surveillance, video EEG for uncertain events, standard antiseizure or spasm therapy when indicated, empirical movement/behavior treatment; 4-aminopyridine is investigational.

Epidemiology and Risk Factors

Rarity and case ascertainment

Ultra-rare, without a population prevalence estimate. Current evidence for SYT1-associated Baker-Gordon syndrome comes from small clinically ascertained series rather than population-based surveillance. The initial SYT1 case series included 11 affected individuals identified through clinicians and genomics research groups after recognition of similar unexplained neurodevelopmental presentations, with exome or genome sequencing performed across 10 centres [PMID:30107533]. A later age-focused SYT1 cohort analyzed 40 affected individuals from infancy to adulthood, but this remains a cohort count rather than a prevalence denominator [PMID:41332143].

Case counts reflect ascertainment pathways. Reported numbers should be interpreted as recognized cases, not disease frequency. The 11-person SYT1 series was enriched by clinician referral, genomic sequencing, and research-group matching [PMID:30107533], while a Brazilian report described a single proband as the first identified patient from Brazil and summarized 9 DECIPHER entries with missense SYT1 variants and a Baker-Gordon phenotype [PMID:38283597]. The Brazilian proband was simplex [PMID:38283597], consistent with the de novo pattern emphasized in SYT1-associated reports rather than an inherited familial risk estimate.

Variant recurrence supports recognizability, not prevalence. In the 11-person SYT1 series, recurrent de novo amino-acid substitutions were reported—M303K in 1 individual, D304G in 1, D366E in 3, I368T in 4, and N371K in 2 [PMID:30107533]. In the DECIPHER-based summary, the c.1103T>C, p.Ile368Thr variant accounted for 3 of 9 sequence-variant entries [PMID:38283597]. These recurrent findings help define the molecular case series but should not be converted into population frequency or genotype-specific risk without unbiased ascertainment.

Demographic signals are descriptive. A DECIPHER-based summary reported an approximately 2:1 male predominance among both SYT1 sequence-variant cases and copy-number-variant cases involving SYT1 [PMID:38283597], whereas the 40-person SYT1 cohort was nearly sex-balanced, with 19 of 40 affected individuals female [PMID:41332143]. This contrast suggests that sex ratios in available reports may reflect small numbers and ascertainment differences rather than a confirmed sex-specific risk.

Nomenclature caveat. Some literature uses “Baker-Gordon syndrome” in the CLIFAHDD context: one NALCN/UNC80 cross-sectional cohort reported 34 patients with CLIFAHDD/Baker-Gordon syndrome among 51 individuals with pathogenic or likely pathogenic NALCN or UNC80 variants [PMID:40048676]. These cases should be treated as a distinct nomenclature/case-definition issue and not pooled with SYT1-associated Baker-Gordon case counts.

De novo dominant genetic risk

Typical inheritance context. SYT1-associated Baker-Gordon syndrome is usually encountered as a simplex neurodevelopmental disorder identified through exome or genome sequencing, with recurrent de novo missense SYT1 substitutions reported across affected individuals [PMID:30107533]. The Brazilian report similarly described a de novo SYT1 missense variant in a proband with Baker-Gordon syndrome [PMID:38283597].

Family history pattern. Published cases support counseling families that an affected child may have unaffected parents and no recognizable prior family history [PMID:30107533, 38283597]. In the Brazilian simplex case, the proband was the second child of healthy, non-consanguineous parents, had healthy sisters, and had no reported prior family history [PMID:38283597].

Recurrence counseling implications. The key counseling point is that confirmed pathogenic SYT1 missense variants in Baker-Gordon syndrome are often de novo, so parental testing is important to distinguish a new dominant event from an inherited variant [PMID:30107533, 38283597]. When both parents are clinically unaffected and the variant is absent on parental testing, the available reports support a low observed familial recurrence pattern, although families should still receive genetics-based recurrence counseling because published ascertainment has largely involved simplex cases rather than multiplex pedigrees [PMID:30107533, 38283597].

Recurrent SYT1 variant hotspots

Recurrent hotspots are a consistent epidemiologic signal. In an 11-person SYT1 case series ascertained across 10 sequencing centers, pathogenic de novo amino acid substitutions clustered at a small number of residues: M303K in 1 individual, D304G in 1, D366E in 3, I368T in 4, and N371K in 2 [PMID:30107533]. This pattern indicates recurrence of specific substitutions across independently ascertained affected individuals rather than a broad distribution of private missense variants [PMID:30107533].

I368T is the most repeatedly observed substitution. The recurrent p.Ile368Thr variant was present in 4 of 11 individuals in the multicenter case series [PMID:30107533], represented 6 affected participants in a functional–phenotype correlation cohort [PMID:39481209], and accounted for 3 of 9 DECIPHER Baker-Gordon phenotype entries with missense SYT1 sequence variants in a DECIPHER-based summary [PMID:38283597]. A reported Brazilian proband with p.Ile368Thr had a simplex family history with healthy, non-consanguineous parents and no prior family history, extending the same recurrent variant to an additional geographic context without supporting a founder interpretation [PMID:38283597].

Other residues also recur, supporting constrained functional hotspots. D366E and N371K recurred in multiple individuals in the 11-person series [PMID:30107533], while additional putative pathogenic substitutions represented as single-participant genotypes in a later functional cohort included T196K, E209K, E219Q, M303V, S309P, Y365C, and G369D [PMID:39481209]. Across these reports, recurrence is best interpreted clinically as enrichment at functionally constrained SYT1 residues rather than as evidence for shared ancestry or a founder effect [PMID:30107533, 39481209].

Population data support pathogenic-residue constraint. In a gnomAD v4.10 population variant review, no missense variants were present at the putative pathogenic SYT1 residues highlighted by the authors [PMID:39481209]. This absence of population missense variation at implicated residues strengthens the clinical interpretation that recurrent substitutions such as I368T occur at constrained sites with high disease relevance [PMID:39481209].

Demographic patterns in reported cohorts

Age distribution. Published SYT1-associated cohort data show ascertainment across a broad age span, from infancy to adulthood: in a 40-person cohort, age at participation ranged from 0.2 to 34.3 years, including 2 infants, 24 children, and 14 adolescents/adults [PMID:41332143]. This pattern indicates that affected individuals are reported throughout childhood and into adult follow-up, although children made up the largest subgroup in that cohort [PMID:41332143].

Sex distribution. The largest reported SYT1-associated cohort had a nearly balanced sex distribution, with 19 of 40 individuals female [PMID:41332143]. In contrast, a small DECIPHER-based summary of 9 Baker-Gordon phenotype cases with missense SYT1 sequence variants reported an approximately 2:1 male predominance [PMID:38283597]. The same DECIPHER summary separately noted an approximately 2:1 male predominance among copy-number-variant cases involving SYT1 [PMID:38283597].

Clinical interpretation. Current data do not establish sex as a risk factor for Baker-Gordon syndrome or broader SYT1-associated neurodevelopmental disorder. The apparent male predominance in DECIPHER should be interpreted cautiously because it comes from small, database-derived sequence-variant and copy-number-variant groups [PMID:38283597], and it contrasts with the nearly balanced sex distribution in the larger 40-person cohort [PMID:41332143]. Sequence-variant cases and SYT1-involving copy-number-variant cases should be kept distinct when describing demographic patterns [PMID:38283597].

Prenatal and neonatal associations

Prenatal clues. In the largest summarized cohort of individuals with SYT1-associated neurodevelopmental disorder, pregnancy concerns were reported in 19/35 pregnancies with available data, and reduced fetal movements were reported in 8/35 [PMID:41332143]. These findings should be interpreted as possible early manifestations or ascertainment clues in affected individuals, not as established causal prenatal risk factors [PMID:41332143].

Neonatal respiratory association. Neonatal admission to a NICU or special care baby unit was reported in 8/30 newborns with available data, usually because of respiratory difficulties [PMID:41332143]. In a clinician-facing history, unexplained neonatal respiratory distress or need for higher-level neonatal observation may support early suspicion when later neurodevelopmental features emerge, but current evidence does not establish neonatal respiratory illness as a cause of Baker-Gordon syndrome [PMID:41332143].

Etiology and Pathogenesis

SYT1-mediated synaptic vesicle cycling

SYT1 is the core presynaptic disease gene in Baker-Gordon syndrome. Reported cases are most often caused by heterozygous de novo SYT1 protein-altering variants, including recurrent or novel missense variants identified by trio sequencing [PMID:25705886, 30107533, 32362337, 35101335, 38321119, 38283597, 39049490, 41332143], an in-frame insertion such as Lys367dup [PMID:35101335], a likely pathogenic C2A-domain V184A variant in a severely affected newborn [PMID:37930470], and a de novo intragenic exon 6–8 deletion predicted to remove the cytoplasmic linker and entire C2A domain while retaining C2B [PMID:42750022]. Most missense variants affect highly conserved residues in the C2A or C2B domains, with many early and functionally studied variants clustering in the C2B Ca2+-binding/membrane-interacting region [PMID:30107533, 32362337, 35101335, 39481209, 38283597]. Structural-disruption data also support SYT1 dosage or regulatory loss: a t(7;12) breakpoint in the SYT1 second intron/5′ UTR was interpreted as separating the coding region from its promoter and was associated with about 70% reduced SYT1 expression in PBMCs [PMID:37916192], whereas a complex rearrangement disrupting SYT1 intron 9 was interpreted as interrupting or abolishing the C2B domain and supporting haploinsufficiency or dominant-negative loss of functional SYT1 [PMID:41438914]. Contiguous 12q21 deletion mapping further narrowed a critical interval containing SYT1 and PPP1R12A as candidate genes [PMID:32633079].

C2-domain disruption impairs Ca2+-triggered vesicle fusion. C2B variants at Met303, Asp304, Asp366, Ile368, and Asn371 map to the Ca2+-binding region; D304G and D366E affect residues directly contacting bound Ca2+, I368T affects a membrane-penetrating residue, and N371 influences Ca2+-binding loop conformation [PMID:30107533]. Biophysical studies showed graded reductions in Ca2+-sensitive membrane binding, faster Ca2+ release from protein-lipid complexes, and impaired Ca2+-dependent membrane penetration by C2B and, through interdomain effects, C2A [PMID:32362337]. Molecular-dynamics analyses support variant-specific mechanisms, including reduced Ca2+ binding time for D304G [PMID:30107533], structural transitions with M303K [PMID:30107533], altered C2A structural features or surface charge [PMID:35101335], altered C2B calcium-loop mobility [PMID:35101335], and possible altered membrane interaction for Lys367dup and Gly369Asp [PMID:35101335]. Together, these findings support defective coupling of Ca2+ sensing to synaptic-vesicle membrane engagement rather than nonspecific failure of protein production or targeting for most variants [PMID:30107533, 32362337, 38321119, 39481209].

Dominant-negative impairment of evoked exocytosis is a recurring functional driver. The original I368T variant localized to nerve terminals and was expressed at approximately endogenous wild-type levels, but slowed synaptic-vesicle fusion kinetics and altered endocytosis kinetics in cultured hippocampal neurons [PMID:25705886]. In larger functional series, D304G, D366E, I368T, and N371K slowed evoked synaptic-vesicle exocytosis without reducing the total recycling vesicle pool [PMID:30107533], and multiple C2A and C2B variants produced graded, variant-specific dominant-negative impairment of evoked vesicle exocytosis [PMID:39481209]. Most assayed variants did not significantly impair nerve-terminal expression, presynaptic localization, or vesicular targeting [PMID:39481209], although M303K showed lower expression and reduced resting nerve-terminal localization [PMID:30107533], L159R showed lower synaptic SYT1 levels with more diffuse axonal localization [PMID:39481209], and D304G/D366E showed impaired relocalization to nerve terminals after stimulation, suggesting defective endocytic retrieval or trafficking [PMID:30107533]. Increasing extracellular Ca2+ partly improved mutant release rates but did not fully normalize them relative to wild-type at the same Ca2+ concentration [PMID:30107533], supporting impaired Ca2+ coupling rather than simple Ca2+ insufficiency.

Genotype-function relationships are clinically relevant. C2B variants generally have stronger functional effects than C2A variants: all tested C2B variants in one study significantly slowed the initial exocytic rate, whereas tested C2A variants produced more modest, nonsignificant slowing in that assay [PMID:39481209]. M303V and S309P reduced the total synaptic-vesicle recycling pool [PMID:39481209], and M303V, S309P, and I368T reduced the readily releasable vesicle pool, whereas tested C2A variants did not [PMID:39481209]. D366E/D365E showed milder biochemical, dominant-negative, synaptic, and clinical effects than D304G/D303G and I368T/I367T [PMID:32362337], and D366E had the mildest exocytosis defect in another series, correlating with milder motor delay and absence of early-onset movement disorder [PMID:30107533]. Across an expanded cohort, all individuals with C2A variants had mild or moderate adaptive impairment and no early-onset movement disorder, while the most severe phenotypes were associated with C2B variants, although not all C2B variants were severe [PMID:35101335].

SNARE-interface and clamping defects broaden the SYT1 mechanism beyond slowed synchronous release. Pro401Leu, located at the bottom of C2B near arginine residues forming the primary Syt1-SNARE interface, was proposed to impair clamping by altering positioning of nearby SNARE-interacting residues [PMID:38321119]. The orthologous Syt1 P400L protein was normally expressed and synaptically targeted, but selectively increased asynchronous release during single action potentials and stimulus trains, slowed post-train tail-current decay, and increased spontaneous miniature EPSC frequency about fourfold compared with wild-type rescue [PMID:38321119]. Key measures of synchronous evoked release, including rise time, release probability, train rundown, readily releasable pool size, and vesicle recruitment rate, were largely preserved, indicating a desynchronization and release-clamping phenotype rather than global exocytosis failure [PMID:38321119]. The same variant acted dominantly in wild-type neurons and produced activity-dependent reductions in dendrite length and synapse number [PMID:38321119]. Other predicted interface-level mechanisms include possible perturbation of SYT1-SNARE/SNAP-25 interaction for Asn341Ser [PMID:35101335].

Neuronal excitability pathways

Postsynaptic Ras/ERK and related synaptic signaling. SYNGAP1-related disease is the clearest non-SYT1 example of altered excitability through postsynaptic signaling: SYNGAP1 encodes a RAS/RAP GTP-activating protein, and functional data showed that wild-type SYNGAP1 reduced activity-dependent phosphorylated ERK in cortical pyramidal cells whereas patient-associated mutant constructs did not, supporting loss of function and haploinsufficiency [PMID:23161826]. Multiple cohorts identified de novo truncating, frameshift, splice-altering, missense, or whole-gene deletion events, with convergence on a haploinsufficiency mechanism [PMID:23708187, 26079862, 26989088, 30541864]. A noncanonical splice-region variant, c.1676+5G>A, activated a cryptic donor site and produced frameshifted premature-termination transcripts, demonstrating loss of normal full-length SYNGAP1 from the variant allele [PMID:28576131]. Clinically, SYNGAP1 disruption repeatedly presented with intellectual disability and epilepsy or epileptic encephalopathy [PMID:23161826, 23708187, 26989088, 30541864], with 6p21.3/6p21.32 microdeletions including SYNGAP1 producing similar epileptology and associated features to intragenic variants [PMID:23687080, 26079862, 30541864]. Phenotypic variability is clinically important: microdeletion cases had earlier seizure onset and severe intellectual disability in one cohort [PMID:30541864], while milder phenotypes with normal intellect or mild intellectual disability were reported in a subset, often with truncating or splice-site variants in exons 1–4 [PMID:30541864].

Phosphatase dysregulation. PPP2R5D-related disease reflects altered PP2A substrate regulation rather than a primary ion-channel defect: de novo missense variants affecting the B56δ regulatory subunit clustered in a conserved acidic loop, with recurrent p.E198K and p.E200K variants [PMID:26168268, 25972378, 26576547, 34946857]. Functional data showed mutant B56δ proteins were stable but deficient in binding PP2A A and C subunits and uncoupled from phosphatase activity, supporting a dominant-negative model with impaired dephosphorylation of anchored substrates [PMID:26168268]. Structural and modeling data placed disease-associated PPP2R5D variants in substrate-recognition or holoenzyme-interface regions, suggesting altered substrate positioning, electrostatic disruption near the active site, or disturbed A–C subunit binding [PMID:25972378, 26576547]. PPP3CA-related disease adds a calcineurin axis: de novo PPP3CA variants were linked to severe neurodevelopmental disease with seizures [PMID:28942967], while Baker-Gordon–relevant missense variants in the auto-inhibitory domain increased calcineurin signaling in yeast, consistent with gain of function [PMID:29432562]. Other PPP3CA developmental and epileptic encephalopathy variants cluster in regulatory or catalytic domains, with reported mechanisms including suspected haploinsufficiency from unstable truncated protein [PMID:30455226], constitutive enzyme activation with reduced protein expression [PMID:30254215], loss of the autoinhibitory domain [PMID:36158964], and domain-dependent effects on severity [PMID:39305655, 40548073].

Ion-channel and transporter mechanisms. NALCN-associated CLIFAHDD/Baker-Gordon presentations are best framed as altered sodium leak-channel excitability: autosomal dominant gain-of-function NALCN variants affect a channelosome that regulates resting membrane potential [PMID:40048676]. Domain-specific effects are clinically relevant, with pore-domain variants associated with premature death, epilepsy, or paroxysmal dystonia in one genotype–phenotype analysis [PMID:40048676], and individual de novo missense variants mapped to pore-forming S5/S6 regions or conserved channel regions predicted to impair gating or channel function [PMID:26763878, 35055596, 35911839]. Potassium-channel disorders show parallel domain specificity: KCNH1 variants affected the S4 voltage sensor or S6 pore-domain helix in patients with developmental delay, hypotonia, and seizures [PMID:26818738], and KCNH5 missense variants in voltage-sensing or pore domains showed an emerging severity gradient, from self-limited drug-responsive epilepsy with p.Arg333His to infantile-onset developmental and epileptic encephalopathy with p.Arg327His and more severe neonatal/early-infantile disease with pore-domain variants [PMID:36307226]. Other excitability mechanisms include SCN2A epilepsy, in which de novo variants were associated with more severe developmental delay [PMID:29996185], CACNA1G missense variants associated with upregulated channel function in infantile-onset syndromic cerebellar ataxia [PMID:31836334], and biallelic SLC12A5/KCC2 variants causing partial loss of neuronal chloride extrusion with residual transporter function, implicating impaired inhibitory chloride homeostasis rather than gross trafficking failure [PMID:27436767].

Receptor-mediated inhibition and G-protein/cAMP signaling. Receptor-level inhibitory pathways are represented by GABRA1-associated epilepsy, in which 10 of 11 reported heterozygous pathogenic variants were de novo [PMID:32102148], and by a de novo pathogenic GABBR2 p.G693W variant associated with paroxysmal limb dystonias [PMID:35414446]. G-protein signaling also contributes to excitability phenotypes: de novo GNAO1 missense variants were reported in patients spanning epileptic encephalopathy to involuntary movements with severe developmental delay, and structural evaluation predicted impaired Gαo function for three variants [PMID:25966631]. ADCY5-related disease involves cAMP pathway dysregulation at a recurrent codon, with heterozygous R418 variants including p.R418W, p.R418G, and p.R418Q; p.R418W kindreds had more severe dyskinesias, axial hypotonia, and motor developmental delay than the p.R418G or p.R418Q probands in one series [PMID:27061943].

Synaptic-vesicle machinery and kinase-linked synaptic pathways. STXBP1-related encephalopathy illustrates synaptic-vesicle release machinery involvement: disease-causing variants included missense, protein-truncating, splice-site, deletion, frameshift, duplication, and in-frame deletion classes across a 534-person cohort, with recurrent hotspots such as p.Arg406, p.Arg292, and p.Arg551 but no recurrent group showing a distinct electroclinical syndrome after correction for multiple testing [PMID:35190816]. Smaller STXBP1 series similarly reported de novo heterozygous sequence variants or whole-gene deletion in children with encephalopathy and epilepsy [PMID:36440324, 37908909]. DNM1 variants implicate vesicle-cycle GTPase function: de novo G-domain variants were associated with early-onset epileptic encephalopathy, West syndrome, Lennox-Gastaut syndrome, or developmental delay without seizures, with structural or in vitro data suggesting altered nucleotide binding, dimer formation, GTPase activity, or protein expression depending on the allele [PMID:26611353, 39954101]. Kinase-linked synaptic mechanisms include de novo PAK1 missense variants predicted to alter protein stability or ATP-related interactions, with one mosaic p.Leu470Pro case and peak PAK1 expression in developing brain regions [PMID:41124873], and CDK19 loss-of-function variants that failed to rescue Drosophila Cdk8 loss; neuronal knockdown caused seizures and reduced boutons and synapses at larval neuromuscular junctions, supporting impaired synaptic development or maintenance as a possible disease mechanism [PMID:32330417].

Gene-regulatory mechanisms

Chromatin and epigenetic dosage mechanisms. KANSL1-related disease is framed as haploinsufficiency, with either 17q21.31 deletion or intragenic KANSL1 point mutation considered sufficient for the full phenotype [PMID:26424144]. A de novo CHD8 frameshift was supported by RNA and protein studies as causing insufficient CHD8 dosage rather than a gain-of-function effect [PMID:26789910]. BRPF1-related syndromic intellectual disability showed a more specific chromatin mark defect, with patient variants impairing histone H3 lysine 23 acetylation and Brpf1-knockout mouse lines showing similar acetylation deficiency [PMID:27939640]. Additional chromatin/BAF-associated presentations include a de novo truncating KAT6A variant [PMID:30105118], a DPF2 missense variant framed within BAF chromatin-remodeling disease [PMID:31706665], de novo PHF21A variants modeled by phf21ab-knockout zebrafish with reduced forebrain, midbrain, and whole-brain area [PMID:40784402], and ANKRD11 frameshift/nonsense or activation-domain missense variants causing KBG or Coffin-Siris-like phenotypes after BAF-complex and SOX11 causes were excluded in Coffin-Siris-like cases [PMID:28250421]. A homozygous TLK2 p.Lys55Glu variant was proposed to reduce nuclear TLK2 activity by impairing nuclear import, although the authors emphasized that functional confirmation is still needed [PMID:31558842].

Developmental transcription-factor networks. SOX11 provides a clear example of a developmental transcription-factor mechanism: de novo SOX11 mutations caused a mild Coffin-Siris phenotype, sox11a/b knockdown disrupted zebrafish brain development, and SOX11 was placed downstream of the PAX6-BAF transcriptional network [PMID:24886874]. Larger SOX11 series identified de novo missense, nonsense, and whole-gene deletion variants [PMID:39333428], and endocrine-focused reports linked deleterious SOX11 variants to idiopathic hypogonadotropic hypogonadism and other pituitary hormone deficiencies, suggesting hypothalamic and pituitary-level developmental effects [PMID:39290158, 42168980]. SOX4 variants clustered in the HMG DNA-binding domain, abolished DNA binding and reporter transactivation, and were supported by fetal expression in neurogenic regions plus Xenopus knockdown causing reduced brain and body size [PMID:30661772]. FOXG1 illustrates dosage sensitivity in both directions: mosaic duplication including FOXG1 was associated with West syndrome [PMID:21910242], whereas de novo FOXG1 sequence variants included forkhead-domain missense and frameshift variants, with milder outcome reported for the forkhead missense case than for truncating cases [PMID:34964776]. Other transcriptional regulators in this group include recurrent de novo NACC1 p.Arg298Trp, including one mosaic individual, consistent with an allele-specific hotspot in a transcriptional repressor [PMID:28132692]; de novo NEUROD2 p.Glu130Gln in epileptic spasms [PMID:36494631]; and de novo TBL1XR1 p.Gly29Asp attributed to West syndrome with global developmental delay [PMID:37171308].

Core transcription machinery and transcriptional signaling. POLR2A-related disease is mechanistically important because variants in RNA polymerase II subunit RPB1 can act through different transcriptional effects: missense variants with mild structural impact were proposed to create a malfunctioning polymerase with dominant-negative interference, whereas complete loss-of-function variants were interpreted as reducing functional polymerase dosage and were associated with milder phenotypes in one cohort [PMID:31353023]. A second POLR2A cohort found ultra-rare variants across Clamp, Dock, Cleft, Trigger loop, Jaw, and C-terminal domains, with yeast assays showing conditional growth, protein-folding or assembly, and transcription-related defects for subsets of variants [PMID:33665635]. The inherited POLR2A C-terminal frameshift was predicted to escape nonsense-mediated decay and remove multiple heptapeptide repeats, with transmission from a mildly affected mother supporting variable expressivity or reduced penetrance [PMID:33665635]. Single-case POLR2A reports add domain-specific support, including de novo p.Ile457Thr [PMID:35461703] and de novo p.Glu1289Lys predicted to disrupt interaction with transcription-elongation factor TFIIS and reduce elongation fidelity/rate [PMID:35689525]. CDK19 variants similarly implicate transcriptional regulation: patient-associated p.Tyr32His and p.Thr196Ala behaved as null alleles in Drosophila Cdk8 rescue assays, while reference human CDK19 rescued Cdk8 loss [PMID:32330417]. SIAH1 variants, including de novo missense and predicted loss-of-function alleles, were linked to loss of Wnt stimulatory activity and dysregulated Wnt/β-catenin signaling [PMID:32430360, 40156476].

Nucleic-acid binding, RNA processing, and post-transcriptional control. PURA-related disease is supported by de novo pathogenic sequence variants across PUR domains without a consistent severity relationship by variant class or domain [PMID:29150892], by de novo 5q31.2-q31.3 microdeletion including PURA [PMID:29619234], and by an inherited PURA p.Arg99Leu variant in the nucleic-acid-binding domain predicted to have only a minor binding effect, consistent with a milder familial phenotype [PMID:39131487]. HNRNPU disease is framed as de novo heterozygous loss-of-function or haploinsufficiency and as a contributor to the 1q43-q44 deletion phenotype [PMID:28815871, 29858110]. PUM1 shows post-transcriptional dosage sensitivity: deletions narrowed the shared interval to PUM1, severe de novo missense variants affected the PUMILIO homology domain, patient fibroblasts showed reduced PUM1 protein with unchanged mRNA, and PUM1 target transcripts/proteins were increased [PMID:29474920]. TRA2B variants define an isoform-mediated dominant-negative splicing disorder, with 5′ clustered loss-of-function variants decreasing canonical Tra2β-1, increasing shorter Tra2β-3, and impairing CHEK1 exon 3 inclusion [PMID:36549593]. GEMIN5-related disease in two families involved inherited compound heterozygous variants in the tetratricopeptide repeat-like domain [PMID:37479787], and AMMECR1 p.Gly177Asp retained nuclear localization but showed abnormal non-uniform nuclear expression and fewer GFP-positive cells in expression assays [PMID:27811305].

Restricted hotspots and RNA biogenesis phenotypes. ACTL6B dominant disease is notable for recurrent de novo missense variation, especially p.Gly343 in 24 independent families, and cellular studies showed nucleolar ACTL6B localization with knockdown causing accumulation of 30S and 21S pre-rRNA, supporting impaired pre-rRNA processing and ribosome biogenesis [PMID:39275948]. ATN1-related CHEDDA syndrome showed a highly restricted mutation spectrum, with rare de novo missense or in-frame indel variants in the exon 7 HX motif [PMID:34212383], and an additional infantile developmental and epileptic encephalopathy case carried de novo ATN1 p.His1052Pro [PMID:38262122]. These hotspot and isoform-specific disorders are clinically useful because pathogenicity may depend less on broad gene disruption and more on the precise residue, motif, or transcript context affected [PMID:34212383, 36549593, 39275948].

Cellular homeostasis pathways

Intracellular trafficking and organelle clearance. Recessive AP4S1 loss-of-function disease provides direct cellular evidence for impaired adaptor-protein trafficking: patient fibroblasts showed reduction of all AP-4 subunits, loss of AP-4 complex assembly, and abolished membrane recruitment of tepsin [PMID:25552650]. PACS2-related disease similarly centers on disturbed sorting biology, with a recurrent de novo c.625G>A/p.Glu209Lys variant impairing autoregulatory control of interactions between the PACS2 cargo/furin-binding region and client proteins [PMID:29656858]; additional series and family reports support recurrence of the same heterozygous missense variant, with de novo status shown where parental testing was available [PMID:34405643, 38545008]. Endolysosomal dysfunction is supported by CLCN6 p.Glu200Ala, which uncoupled ClC-6 chloride transport from proton antiport and caused autophagosome accumulation from impaired autophagosome–lysosome fusion in heterologous systems [PMID:33590434]. ATP6V1A adds a vesicular-acidification-associated genetic node, with a 10-child series showing predominantly de novo heterozygous missense variants and mosaicism in one child [PMID:41233134].

GPI-anchor and metabolic biosynthesis. PIGA and PIGS disorders link developmental epileptic encephalopathy to impaired GPI-anchor biosynthesis with measurable cell-surface consequences: PIGA-mutated patients had reduced GPI-anchored proteins on granulocyte flow cytometry, and mutant PIGA only partially or hardly restored GPI-anchored protein expression in deficient cells, with severity tracking residual activity [PMID:24706016]. In PIGS disease, compound heterozygous variants were associated with reduced CD59 and slightly reduced FLAER binding on granulocytes, indicating decreased GPI-anchored proteins in peripheral white blood cells [PMID:32612635]. Metabolic-biosynthetic mechanisms are also represented by SLC13A5, where biallelic variants affecting sodium-binding residues implicate disrupted neuronal citrate transport/metabolism [PMID:24995870], and by homozygous UGDH p.Ala44Val, affecting an enzyme that generates UDP-glucuronate for glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis [PMID:39460648].

Protein stability, cytoskeletal integrity, and myelination. SPATA5 illustrates a protein-stability mechanism: modeled disease variants were predicted to destabilize the AAA ATPase hexamer or subunit interface, reducing enzymatic activity [PMID:27683084]. Cytoskeletal and myelination biology is supported by SPTAN1, where a de novo in-frame p.Glu2270del variant was linked to early-onset intractable seizures with severe hypomyelination and widespread brain volume reduction [PMID:22429196]. For SZT2, the evidence provided is primarily genetic rather than functional, with compound heterozygous variants—including truncating, splice-site, and missense alleles—identified in three patients with early-onset epileptic encephalopathy, and exon 20 skipping causing a premature stop codon in one splice variant [PMID:28556953].

Ribosome and ribonucleoprotein-complex biology. ACTL6B links Baker-Gordon presentations to ribosome biogenesis through recurrent de novo monoallelic missense variants, most often at p.Gly343, and ACTL6B knockdown causing accumulation of 30S and 21S pre-rRNA species, supporting defective pre-rRNA processing [PMID:39275948]. GEMIN5-related cases add a primarily genetic association involving compound heterozygous variants in the tetratricopeptide repeat-like domain inherited from unaffected parents, without a reported cellular functional assay in the provided data [PMID:37479787].

Ubiquitin-related regulation. SIAH1, an E3 ubiquitin ligase gene, has convergent genetic and functional support: five unrelated individuals had de novo monoallelic variants with modeling effects on conserved functional sites and in vitro loss of Wnt stimulatory activity [PMID:32430360], and a later series identified SIAH1 variants in eight unrelated families, including six predicted loss-of-function variants [PMID:40156476]. TRIM8 disease evidence includes a de novo nonsense variant with renal biopsy showing absent or markedly reduced TRIM8 expression and strong SOCS1 expression, supporting altered TRIM8/SOCS1 biology in kidney involvement [PMID:32193649]. HECW2 currently contributes mainly gene-level evidence in the provided reports, with pathogenic or likely splicing variants identified in infants with developmental and epileptic encephalopathy [PMID:40705299, 40879666]. OTUD5 adds an X-linked ubiquitin-regulatory association, with a hemizygous missense variant segregating through a carrier mother and heterozygous sister [PMID:41838201].

Genetic architecture

Established SYT1/Baker-Gordon evidence supports a predominantly heterozygous, de novo architecture. Early clinically recognized cases had normal karyotypes and no family history, leaving etiology unresolved before gene-level testing [PMID:6182156]. In molecularly defined SYT1-associated neurodevelopmental disorder/Baker-Gordon syndrome, included individuals had confirmed de novo protein-altering SYT1 variants, while truncating variants, an inherited likely nonpathogenic variant, a homozygous variant, and an alternative molecular diagnosis were excluded; 2 of 30 variants remained VUS pending further evidence [PMID:41332143]. A reported proband with classic Baker-Gordon phenotype carried a de novo heterozygous SYT1 c.1103T>C, p.Ile368Thr variant, and DECIPHER entries summarized in that report were heterozygous and listed as de novo, with most missense variants in C2B and one in C2A [PMID:38283597]. A novel de novo heterozygous intragenic SYT1 exon 6–8 deletion was classified as likely pathogenic, although breakpoint-level junction data and RNA/protein confirmation were not available [PMID:42750022]. Structural disruption also supports SYT1 causality: a t(7;12) breakpoint separated the SYT1 coding region from its promoter and reduced SYT1 expression by 70% in PBMCs [PMID:37916192], and a complex t(5;12) with inv(12) disrupted SYT1 intron 9 in a child with a compatible dominant neurodevelopmental phenotype [PMID:41438914]. By contrast, an earlier 12q21 deletion series narrowed a 1.6-Mb critical region containing SYT1 and PPP1R12A and should be treated as candidate-region rather than single-gene SYT1 proof [PMID:32633079].

Copy-number, structural, UPD, and dosage findings are important in Baker-Gordon-like and overlapping developmental epileptic encephalopathies, but they do not all define SYT1-Baker-Gordon syndrome. De novo SYNGAP1 dosage loss has been reported as a 50-kb 6p21.3 deletion involving four genes with SYNGAP1 considered responsible [PMID:23687080], as a 0.39-Mb deletion encompassing SYNGAP1 plus 18 genes in a de novo series [PMID:26079862], and as 6p21.32 microdeletions in a larger SYNGAP1 cohort in which all tested variants were de novo [PMID:30541864]. KANSL1 haploinsufficiency was caused by either 17q21.31 deletion or intragenic KANSL1 point mutation, with point mutations considered sufficient for the full phenotype [PMID:26424144]. A de novo 2.2-Mb 5q31.2-q31.3 deletion including PURA was confirmed by parental FISH [PMID:29619234], and heterozygous PUM1 deletions had a minimal shared interval containing only PUM1 [PMID:29474920]. STXBP1-related disorder showed a broad structural spectrum including whole/partial gene deletions, duplications, frameshift, splice, missense, in-frame deletion, and one synonymous de novo variant [PMID:35190816]. Copy-number gain and imprinting/UPD can also complicate interpretation: a mosaic 14q11.2-q12 gain encompassing FOXG1 was carried on a mosaic chromosome-14-derived supernumerary marker chromosome in a patient with maternal UPD14 [PMID:21910242], and MAGEL2 frameshift diagnoses required consideration of imprinting after routine inheritance models did not explain the phenotype [PMID:29581464].

De novo dominant sequence variants, hotspots, and mosaicism recur across the overlapping neurodevelopmental literature. PPP2R5D-associated Baker-Gordon syndrome involved heterozygous p.Glu198Lys or p.Glu200Lys variants, including confirmed de novo cases without sequencing evidence of mosaicism [PMID:25972378], and de novo PPP2R5D p.Glu198Lys was also identified as a second diagnosis in a girl with fragile X syndrome [PMID:34946857]. ACTL6B dominant disease was associated with de novo monoallelic missense variants, with p.Gly343 emerging as a recurrent hotspot in 24 independent families [PMID:39275948]. NACC1 disease showed the same recurrent de novo p.Arg298Trp variant in all seven affected individuals, including one mosaic individual [PMID:28132692], and CHEDDA syndrome showed a restricted de novo ATN1 exon 7 “HX motif” mutation spectrum [PMID:34212383]. Other de novo-dominant architectures include SCN2A variants, with de novo cases more often associated with developmental delay [PMID:29996185]; ATP6V1A de novo heterozygous missense variants, including one mosaic child [PMID:41233134]; PAK1 de novo missense variants, including one mosaic variant with 13.4% variant allele fraction [PMID:41124873]; PPP3CA de novo variants, including frameshift, missense, and a de novo intragenic inversion [PMID:40548073]; and NEUROD2 p.E130Q, for which recurrence risk was counseled as less than 1% in that family [PMID:36494631]. STXBP1 had multiple recurrent hotspots, especially p.Arg406, p.Arg292, and p.Arg551, but recurrent groups did not define a robust electroclinical subtype after correction for multiple testing [PMID:35190816].

Autosomal-recessive segregation accounts for several Baker-Gordon-labeled or phenotypically overlapping conditions and changes counseling substantially from the de novo SYT1 model. Reported recessive Baker-Gordon/AP4S1 disease involved compound heterozygous predicted loss-of-function AP4S1 variants in siblings [PMID:25552650], and Baker-Gordon syndrome in two families was associated with parentally inherited compound heterozygous GEMIN5 variants [PMID:37479787]. Other overlapping recessive epileptic/developmental disorders included SLC13A5 variants supported by family cosegregation and an unrelated compound-heterozygous case [PMID:24995870], homozygous WWOX c.606-1G>A with a shared carrier haplotype in two families [PMID:26345274], compound heterozygous SLC12A5 variants with parental segregation and an unaffected carrier sibling [PMID:27436767], biallelic SPATA5 variants with either homozygous segregation or compound heterozygosity [PMID:27683084], compound heterozygous SZT2 variants in three patients including an unexpectedly compound-heterozygous child of consanguineous parents [PMID:28556953], paternal/maternal compound heterozygous PIGS variants in an affected child of unaffected parents [PMID:32612635], a homozygous likely pathogenic TLK2 variant in an affected child of unaffected carrier parents [PMID:31558842], and a homozygous pathogenic UGDH variant in familial infantile epileptic spasms [PMID:39460648].

Familial transmission and recurrence-risk modifiers include X-linked, imprinted, mosaic, and variably expressive dominant mechanisms. X-linked examples include PIGA mutations at Xp22.2 with variable residual activity and severity [PMID:24706016], hemizygous AMMECR1 p.G177D segregating in affected maternal half-brothers with an unaffected carrier mother and no explanatory Xq22.3-Xq23 CNV [PMID:27811305], and hemizygous OTUD5 p.Arg399Trp in an affected male with maternal carrier status and a heterozygous sister [PMID:41838201]. Inherited dominant variable expressivity was documented for POLR2A, where a C-terminal frameshift was transmitted from a mother with delayed speech and mild learning difficulty to an affected child [PMID:33665635]; ANKRD11, where a mother-child pair shared p.Pro2139Leu [PMID:28250421]; PURA, where an affected mother and daughter shared p.Arg99Leu after apparent de novo origin in the mother [PMID:39131487]; and BRPF1, where monoallelic mutations were either inherited or de novo [PMID:27939640]. Clinically, confirmed de novo SYT1 variants generally imply low recurrence risk for tested parents, but observed proband mosaicism in NACC1, ATP6V1A, and PAK1 highlights the need to consider low-level mosaicism when counseling [PMID:28132692, 41233134, 41124873]; biallelic and X-linked segregating disorders instead require carrier testing and family-specific recurrence assessment [PMID:24706016, 24995870, 25552650, 27436767, 27811305, 32612635, 37479787, 41838201].

Clinical Manifestations

Neurodevelopmental and behavioral phenotype

Early developmental course. Neurodevelopmental impairment is usually evident in infancy, often before epilepsy is recognized. In the 11-patient SYT1 series, infantile hypotonia was universal and described as the earliest sign of neurologic impairment [PMID:30107533], and a multi-exon SYT1 deletion case was referred at 13 months for global developmental delay after hypotonia was first noted at about 3 months [PMID:42750022]. Developmental severity ranges widely: severe impairment with lack of independence in daily activities was reported in an 18-year-old with the recurrent I368T variant [PMID:38283597], whereas a milder case had delayed walking at 22 months but later could walk, run, and cycle [PMID:38321119]. Developmental delay is not simply secondary to epilepsy: no diagnosed seizure disorder was present in the original 11-patient cohort [PMID:30107533], epilepsy and developmental regression were absent in three molecularly characterized cases [PMID:32362337], seizures were diagnosed in only 4/22 individuals in an expanded cohort [PMID:35101335], and one patient with severe speech impairment and autism had seizures only later, beginning at 7 years [PMID:41438914].

Cognition and adaptive functioning. Intellectual and adaptive outcomes span a broad range. Severe-to-profound impairment is common, including profound cognitive impairment in the first reported SYT1 case [PMID:25705886], severe intellectual disability with absent speech in an adult case [PMID:38058756], and severe intellectual disability with ineffective social interactions and complete dependence for daily activities in another adult [PMID:38283597]. Standardized adaptive testing in 14 individuals showed scores from borderline/mild intellectual disability to severe/profound impairment, with communication more affected on average than motor, socialization, or daily living domains [PMID:35101335]. In an age-related cohort, Vineland-3 composite scores placed 3/25 individuals in the normal/borderline range, 10/25 in the mildly to moderately impaired range, and 12/25 in the severely to profoundly impaired range [PMID:41332143]. A milder SYT1 case had estimated IQ 64–81, moderate-to-low communication and socialization skills, and adequate-to-high daily living skills [PMID:38321119]. Cognitively normal individuals are described in some overlapping non-SYT1 movement or epilepsy disorders, such as ADCY5-related disease [PMID:27061943] and KCNH5-related epilepsy [PMID:36307226], but this should be treated as comparative context rather than a core Baker-Gordon feature.

Speech and language. Communication impairment is central and often disproportionate. In the 11-patient series, speech and language impairment was severe to profound in 10/11 individuals and 9 used no words, while one milder M303K patient used about 50 words at age 7 years [PMID:30107533]. In the expanded cohort, delayed speech and language development was present in 21/22 individuals, ranging from words or phrases to no words [PMID:35101335]. Molecularly characterized cases showed a similar severity gradient: D304G and I368T had no receptive or expressive language, whereas D366E responded to name and used 4–5 words at age 4 years [PMID:32362337]. Additional reports include absent speech in adulthood [PMID:38058756], severe speech impairment with intellectual disability and autism [PMID:41438914], no meaningful words by 13 months in an SYT1 deletion case [PMID:42750022], and delayed early speech followed by full sentences in a milder school-age presentation [PMID:38321119].

Autism, social engagement, and behavioral dysregulation. Autistic or autism-associated features are recurrent but variable: autism spectrum disorder was reported in a patient with SYT1 disruption [PMID:41438914], pervasive developmental disorder not otherwise specified was diagnosed in a mildly affected child [PMID:38321119], and standardized measures in the age-related cohort showed high autism-associated traits [PMID:41332143]. Social engagement may be impaired, with 6/11 individuals showing poor or absent eye contact, limited interest in social interaction, and absent typical imitation, although some were socially engaged outside agitation episodes [PMID:30107533]. Repetitive, stereotyped, and self-injurious behaviors are common later manifestations. Reported behaviors include repetitive leg kicking, object mouthing, finger chewing, hand biting, head or chest tapping, head butting, and chest beating [PMID:30107533], prominent hand-flapping in an infant with an SYT1 deletion [PMID:42750022], and motor stereotypies in an adult case [PMID:38058756]. Self-injury was reported in 13/22 individuals in the expanded cohort [PMID:35101335] and in 26/29 children and 10/11 adolescents/adults in the age-related cohort [PMID:41332143]. Behavioral dysregulation can include alternating calm and excited/agitated phases with screaming and self-injury [PMID:30107533], rapid mood change and repetitive movements elevated compared with an intellectual-disability comparison group [PMID:35101335], heteroaggression and autoaggression with mood swings [PMID:41438914], and irritability with ineffective social interactions [PMID:38283597].

Sleep and age-related trajectory. Sleep disturbance is a major longitudinal feature. At least 7 patients in the 11-person series had frequent night waking and difficulty returning to sleep into late childhood [PMID:30107533], 12/19 individuals in the expanded cohort had sleep disturbance often evolving from infantile hypersomnia to difficulty initiating and maintaining sleep [PMID:35101335], and the age-related cohort showed increasing sleep disruption in childhood and adolescence/adulthood, with difficulty commencing sleep, frequent waking, nighttime agitation, and prolonged periods without sleep [PMID:41332143]. Developmental acquisition is typically delayed rather than rapidly regressive: in the age-related cohort, unsupported sitting, walking, single words, sentences, and daytime toilet training were each achieved by only subsets of assessed individuals and at delayed median ages [PMID:41332143], while three detailed molecular cases had no developmental regression despite marked impairment [PMID:32362337]. Behavioral manifestations also evolve with age, with self-injurious behavior uncommon in infancy but frequent in childhood and adolescence/adulthood [PMID:41332143].

Epilepsy and electroclinical features

Seizure prevalence is variable and often low. Baker-Gordon syndrome should not be assumed to be a primary developmental and epileptic encephalopathy in every patient. An 11-patient SYT1 series reported no diagnosed seizure disorder despite universal EEG abnormalities [PMID:30107533], and a 3-patient SYT1 study reported absence of epilepsy and developmental regression despite abnormal EEG findings in all three individuals [PMID:32362337]. In the largest expanded SYT1 cohort summarized here, seizures were diagnosed in 4/22 individuals (18%) [PMID:35101335].

Reported seizure types are heterogeneous. In the expanded cohort, seizure phenotypes included absences, tonic-clonic seizures, and infantile spasms [PMID:35101335]. A patient with SYT1 disruption had three documented nonfebrile tonic or tonic-clonic seizures beginning at 7 years of age [PMID:41438914], and another Baker-Gordon case had a medication-induced seizure episode at 4 years [PMID:38283597]. One individual had staring episodes at age 2 years, but EEG showed no abnormalities, supporting caution in labeling paroxysmal behavioral events as epileptic without electroclinical correlation [PMID:38321119]. The available SYT1-specific reports do not establish a recurrent pattern of febrile, reflex, photosensitive, clustered, status epilepticus, or treatment-refractory epilepsy; seizure-positive cases are sparse and individually variable [PMID:35101335, 38283597, 41438914].

EEG abnormalities are common even without epilepsy. The original SYT1 report described extensive neurophysiologic disturbance with low-frequency oscillatory bursts [PMID:25705886]. Subsequent SYT1 reports described universal EEG abnormalities in an 11-patient cohort without diagnosed seizure disorder [PMID:30107533], background slowing with high-amplitude low-frequency slow or spiking activity in all three individuals in one study [PMID:32362337], and EEG abnormality in 17/18 individuals with available data in an expanded cohort, including ictal features, intermittent low-frequency oscillations, and abnormal or generally slowed background activity [PMID:35101335]. One case report noted epilepsy with “peculiar” EEG findings, although the abstract did not specify the pattern [PMID:38058756].

Normal or nondiagnostic EEG also occurs. EEG was normal in a child with staring episodes [PMID:38321119], and another patient had normal EEG studies despite three nonfebrile tonic or tonic-clonic seizures [PMID:41438914]. A child with a de novo multi-exon SYT1 deletion had no epileptiform discharges on EEG at 13 months [PMID:42750022]. Thus, EEG findings in Baker-Gordon syndrome range from normal studies to nonspecific slowing and low-frequency oscillatory or spiking activity, and abnormal EEG alone does not necessarily indicate clinical epilepsy [PMID:30107533, 32362337, 35101335, 38321119, 41438914, 42750022].

Motor phenotype

Early hypotonia and postural delay. Infantile hypotonia is a central early motor sign in SYT1-associated Baker-Gordon syndrome. It was universal in an 11-patient cohort and described as the earliest sign of neurologic impairment [PMID:30107533], present in all three molecularly characterized cases in another series [PMID:32362337], and reported in 19/22 individuals in an expanded cohort [PMID:35101335]. Age-stratified data show hypotonia in 38/40 infants and 32/36 children, but only 5/12 adolescents or adults, suggesting partial attenuation with age in some individuals [PMID:41332143]. The hypotonia is often axial, as illustrated by a child with marked axial hypotonia, mildly reduced limb tone, normal reflexes, and no focal deficits [PMID:42750022], and by reports of severe neonatal hypotonia in a lethal neonatal presentation [PMID:37930470].

Gross motor delay and ambulation. Motor milestones are typically delayed, with severity ranging from mild late walking to persistent non-ambulation. In the expanded SYT1 cohort, motor delay occurred in 18/22 individuals, ranging from walking by 3 years to walking after 5 years or remaining nonambulatory after age 5 [PMID:35101335]. In the 11-patient cohort, independent sitting, when achieved, occurred between 13 months and 4 years, independent walking between 2 and 10 years, and 7 individuals were not walking independently at ages 3–21 years [PMID:30107533]. Age-related cohort data similarly showed unsupported sitting at a median of 18 months and walking at a median of 2.3 years among those who achieved these milestones [PMID:41332143]. Severe presentations include inability to sit or crawl at 13 months [PMID:42750022] and sitting only with assistance at 2 years with dependence for daily activities [PMID:38283597], whereas milder cases include walking at 22 months with later ability to walk, run, and cycle [PMID:38321119] or independent walking at 2 years [PMID:41438914].

Coordination, gait, and tone evolution. Later motor function may be limited by impaired coordination, ataxia, abnormal tone, and reduced adaptive motor skills. Ataxia or impaired fine motor abilities were reported in a milder M303K individual [PMID:30107533], ataxia was included among movement abnormalities in the expanded cohort [PMID:35101335], and age-related data indicate that ataxia can emerge in childhood and persist into adolescence or adulthood [PMID:41332143]. Tone may evolve beyond early hypotonia: some children and adolescents developed hypertonia or stiffness [PMID:41332143], and one molecularly characterized patient developed later childhood spasticity with Babinski signs [PMID:32362337]. Functional motor impact is reflected by reduced Vineland motor scores compared with a monogenic intellectual disability comparison group [PMID:35101335] and by standardized assessment of adaptive motor function in SYT1 cohorts [PMID:39481209].

Involuntary movement disorders. Hyperkinetic movement disorders are a major component of the motor phenotype and appear to become more prominent with age. Movement disorders were reported in 8/22 infants, 30/35 children, and 13/13 adolescents or adults, with hyperkinetic disorders most common [PMID:41332143]. In the expanded cohort, movement abnormalities occurred in 14/21 individuals and included dystonia, chorea, dyskinesia, ataxia, movement-disorder myoclonus, tremor, and stereotypies; more severe cases could show complex hyperkinetic movement disorders [PMID:35101335]. In the 11-patient cohort, post-infantile dystonic and hyperkinetic abnormalities affected 4 patients, ranging from dystonic posturing and mild chorea to severe mixed movement disorder with vocal dystonia and ballismus [PMID:30107533]. Additional reports describe dystonia with lower-limb ballismus and chorea [PMID:32362337], dystonic hand posturing with childhood hyperkinetic movement disorder [PMID:38058756], hyperkinetic movements in a newly reported SYT1 variant [PMID:39049490], and an early-onset dyskinetic movement disorder in the original functional study case [PMID:25705886].

Stereotyped and episodic motor phenomena. Stereotyped motor behaviors, self-injurious motor patterns, and episodic behavioral-motor exacerbations may overlap with the behavioral phenotype. Reported stereotypies include repetitive leg kicking, finger chewing, object mouthing, head or chest tapping, hand biting, head butting, and chest beating [PMID:30107533], prominent hand-flapping in a child with a multi-exon SYT1 deletion [PMID:42750022], and motor stereotypies in an adult case report [PMID:38058756]. Self-injurious behavior was reported in 13/22 individuals in the expanded cohort, most often finger biting or chewing but also head banging and skin picking [PMID:35101335], and age-related data showed self-injury increasing from rare in infancy to frequent in childhood and adolescence/adulthood [PMID:41332143]. Alternating calm and agitated phases lasting minutes to days included increased involuntary movements, screaming, self-injury, object mouthing, and finger or hand chewing [PMID:30107533], and alternating calm/agitated phases with autoaggression and heteroaggression were also described in a SYT1 disruption case [PMID:41438914].

Sensory and neuroimaging findings

Visual phenotype. Ocular and visual abnormalities are among the more recurrent sensory findings in SYT1-related Baker-Gordon syndrome. Strabismus is repeatedly reported, including strabismus in 6 patients and nystagmus in 5 patients in an 11-patient cohort [PMID:30107533], abnormal eye physiology in 20/22 individuals with strabismus/esotropia, nystagmus, hypermetropia, or unspecified visual impairment in an expanded cohort [PMID:35101335], and age-stratified strabismus in 18/36 infants, 21/37 children, and 7/13 adolescents/adults [PMID:41332143]. Nystagmus also persisted across age groups, affecting 15/36 infants, 17/37 children, and 2/13 adolescents/adults [PMID:41332143]. Poor visual attention and later central visual impairment were described in the original SYT1 case series [PMID:30107533], and cerebral visual impairment was reported across age bands in 8/36 infants, 18/37 children, and 9/13 adolescents/adults [PMID:41332143]. Individual reports also describe esotropia and delayed visual maturation [PMID:32362337], visual problems with rod-cone dysfunction [PMID:39049490], and strabismus in BAGOS-overlapping deletion cases [PMID:37916192].

Functional visual testing. Visual evoked potential abnormalities have been documented in SYT1-related disease. One early functional study showed enhanced paired-pulse depression on visual evoked potentials despite normal structural MRI [PMID:25705886], and two assessed patients in a molecular case series had prolonged latency and reduced amplitude [PMID:32362337]. Cerebral visual impairment has also been assessed with dedicated CVI measures in SYT1 cohorts [PMID:39481209], and FCVIQ screening identified 22/27 individuals as at risk for CVI, particularly with difficulties in object and face processing, visual interest, and visually mediated anxiety-related behaviors [PMID:41332143]. Formal descriptions of abnormal saccades are not a recurring feature in the SYT1 reports provided; similarly, cataracts and optic-nerve malformations are not established recurrent findings in Baker-Gordon syndrome from these articles.

Hearing and other sensory testing. Hearing loss is not a prominent or consistent Baker-Gordon feature in the provided SYT1 literature. In one child with a de novo multi-exon SYT1 deletion, hearing evaluation was normal [PMID:42750022], and the complex-rearrangement case reported normal ophthalmologic evaluation, with no sensory deficit highlighted on clinical assessment [PMID:41438914]. By contrast, the sensory burden in Baker-Gordon syndrome is more consistently visual than auditory, with recurrent strabismus, nystagmus, visual impairment, and CVI across cohorts [PMID:30107533, 35101335, 41332143].

Brain MRI: often normal, sometimes nonspecific. Structural MRI can be normal despite severe neurodevelopmental manifestations: the first synaptotagmin-1 functional report described a normal structural brain MRI [PMID:25705886], all three patients in one molecular series had no gross neuroanatomic abnormalities [PMID:32362337], and a complex SYT1-disrupting rearrangement case had no cranial MRI abnormalities [PMID:41438914]. When abnormalities are present, they are heterogeneous rather than pathognomonic. In an expanded SYT1 cohort, MRI abnormalities were reported in 5/15 individuals and included mild diffuse progressive volume loss [PMID:35101335], delayed myelination [PMID:35101335], and a mild periventricular white-matter nodular abnormality [PMID:35101335]. One multi-exon deletion case had a simplified gyral pattern involving the frontal and parietal lobes [PMID:42750022]. Earlier borderline or immature myelination in one SYT1 patient later normalized by age 4 years [PMID:32362337].

Broader deletion imaging findings. Contiguous-gene deletion reports that include SYT1 broaden the possible imaging spectrum but should be interpreted cautiously because of neighboring-gene effects. In 12q21 deletion cases including SYT1 and PPP1R12A, reported neuroimaging included ventriculomegaly or hydrocephalus [PMID:32633079]. Across sequence-level SYT1/Baker-Gordon reports, recurrent corpus callosum malformations, cerebellar dysgenesis or atrophy, heterotopia, pituitary-hypothalamic abnormalities, olfactory-bulb abnormalities, hydrocephalus, or tumor predisposition are not established patterns in the provided literature.

Somatic phenotype and medical comorbidities

Growth and craniofacial pattern. In molecularly confirmed SYT1/Baker-Gordon reports, growth and head size are variable and are not usually defining somatic features. One 11-person cohort reported generally normal growth with orbitofrontal circumference between the 5th and 75th centiles [PMID:30107533], and a multi-exon SYT1 deletion case had normal height, weight, and head circumference at 13 months [PMID:42750022]. Outliers include unexplained tall stature with normal head circumference in one missense-variant case [PMID:38321119], short stature with normal head circumference in a haploinsufficiency case [PMID:41438914], and marked underweight with normal head circumference in an adult whose weight deficit was attributed to dysphagia and limited intake [PMID:38283597]. Facial findings are typically mild and variable, with recurrently described features including prominent high forehead with V-shaped hairline, horizontal low-set eyebrows, mild epicanthus or almond-shaped eyes, fine facial features, short nose with prominent nasal tip, smooth philtrum, and thin upper lip [PMID:30107533]. Individual cases have also shown epicanthal folds, short nose, flat philtrum, and thin upper vermilion [PMID:42750022], or upslanting palpebral fissures, synophrys, anteverted nares, and thick lips [PMID:41438914]. Ectodermal and dental findings are inconsistent, but reported single-case features include thick hair and hypertrichosis [PMID:41438914], persistent fetal finger pads and widely spaced teeth [PMID:38321119], and delayed first tooth eruption [PMID:38283597]. An older clinically defined craniosynostosis–arachnodactyly–abdominal-hernia pattern included severe exophthalmos, maxillary and mandibular hypoplasia, palatal shelf hypertrophy, low-set ears with pliable auricles, thoracic anomalies, and multiple abdominal hernias [PMID:6182156], and should be interpreted separately from the contemporary SYT1 molecular series.

Skeletal, digital, and congenital structural findings. Musculoskeletal findings in SYT1-related Baker-Gordon syndrome are heterogeneous rather than pathognomonic. In a 22-person cohort, musculoskeletal abnormalities occurred in 8/22 and included torticollis, joint hypermobility, talipes, pes planovalgus, progressive contractures, and scoliosis [PMID:35101335]. Smaller reports describe scoliosis in one severely affected child [PMID:32362337], severe scoliosis in an adult case [PMID:38058756], talipes equinovarus in one individual [PMID:32362337], and mild joint hypermobility in a tall-stature presentation [PMID:38321119]. Congenital anomalies were not consistent in the 11-person series, although isolated findings included bilateral talipes in one patient and an atrial septal defect with spontaneous closure in another [PMID:30107533]. Digital or limb differences are generally minor in the SYT1 literature, in contrast to the older arachnodactyly and camptodactyly phenotype reported in the craniosynostosis/hernia syndrome [PMID:6182156].

Feeding, oral-motor, and gastrointestinal comorbidities. Feeding and oral-motor problems are major recurrent medical issues. Age-stratified SYT1 data describe mechanical feeding problems—including sucking, chewing, and swallowing difficulties—as common across age groups, with infantile gastroesophageal reflux and later irregular bowel motility also commonly reported [PMID:41332143]. In the 22-person cohort, abdominal or related symptoms affected 13/22 and included feeding difficulties, gastroesophageal reflux, drooling, constipation, chronic diarrhea, urinary retention, and one case of pancreatitis with pseudocysts [PMID:35101335]. An 11-person series reported difficulty chewing and swallowing solids in 3 children and gastroesophageal reflux in 4 [PMID:30107533]. Individual reports illustrate the severity spectrum: severe reflux and feeding difficulty in one child [PMID:32362337], poor weight gain with disorganized swallowing in another [PMID:32362337], neonatal poor sucking with feeding difficulty in a SYT1-disruption case [PMID:41438914], delayed suction and latching with failure to adapt to breastfeeding in an adult case history [PMID:38283597], and persistent dysphagia for liquids and solids requiring pureed or small-piece foods [PMID:38283597]. A severe neonatal presentation included poor feeding, gastroesophageal reflux, and inability to eat and breathe effectively [PMID:37930470]; gastrostomy use, however, is not a recurrently emphasized intervention in the SYT1 reports provided.

Respiratory, cardiac, renal/urogenital, endocrine, and other medical issues. Respiratory problems are recurrent in SYT1-related disease. An age-related study reported respiratory issues from birth through adulthood, with apnea most common and aspiration, irregular breathing, and recurrent respiratory infections also frequent [PMID:41332143]. Central sleep apnea occurred in 3 patients in one cohort and required supplemental oxygen during infancy [PMID:30107533], while broader cohort data reported respiratory-system abnormalities in 6/22, including sleep apnea, laryngomalacia, hyperventilation with cyanosis, and autonomic dysfunction with hypotension [PMID:35101335]. Cardiac findings appear occasional rather than defining, including an atrial septal defect with spontaneous closure in one cohort patient [PMID:30107533] and atrial septal defect in one of three early molecular cases [PMID:32362337]. Renal and urogenital issues are also mainly case-based, including recurrent urinary tract infections with grade III/IV vesicoureteral reflux [PMID:41438914], recurrent urinary tract infections with strong urine odor since birth [PMID:38283597], and urinary retention within the broader abdominal/autonomic symptom category in one cohort [PMID:35101335]. Endocrine evaluation was normal in a patient assessed for excessive body hair, and pubertal examination showed typical female genitalia with Tanner stage B2P4 [PMID:41438914]. Hematologic or immune abnormalities are not a consistent feature in the provided SYT1 reports; one deletion case had unremarkable routine blood counts, liver and renal function, thyroid testing, and metabolic screening [PMID:42750022]. Other isolated findings include a slight urinary glycine increase [PMID:38283597], mitochondrial respiratory chain deficiencies [PMID:39049490], and rod-cone dysfunction [PMID:39049490].

Complications and Prognosis

Developmental trajectory and behavior

Global developmental prognosis. Developmental impairment is a core long-term feature. The original clinical description included developmental delay and intellectual disability [PMID:6182156], and SYT1/Baker-Gordon reports describe persistent global developmental delay from infancy [PMID:42750022], severe syndromic neurodevelopmental disorder with intellectual disability at 12 years [PMID:41438914], persistence into adulthood at 21 years [PMID:38058756], and ongoing severe impairment with complete dependence in daily activities at 18 years [PMID:38283597]. Severity is heterogeneous, ranging from mild or moderate motor/communication delay in approximately one-third of SYT1 cases to severe or profound developmental impairment [PMID:35101335].

Language, adaptive function, and schooling. Speech and communication are often disproportionately affected, with severe speech impairment reported at 12 years [PMID:41438914] and severe ongoing developmental impairment in late adolescence [PMID:38283597]. Adaptive profiles can be uneven: one 10-year-old had moderate-to-low communication and socialization skills but adequate-to-high daily living skills on Vineland-3 [PMID:38321119]. Educational needs vary; one child required special-needs education because of persistent learning and behavioral difficulties after regular school attendance [PMID:38321119].

Developmental gains versus plateau/regression. Some individuals continue to make gains, including a milder SYT1 case who walked at 28 months and by 4 years could run, feed herself, use a pincer grasp, and had four words [PMID:32362337], and older SYT1 individuals who learned to walk after age 5 years [PMID:35101335]. In the initial SYT1 series, no developmental regression was reported, and the milder D366E case specifically had no regression [PMID:32362337]. Later cohort data support caution: some older children and adolescents developed relative adaptive decline in association with late-childhood neurologic worsening [PMID:35101335], and older age was associated cross-sectionally with lower Vineland-3 adaptive composite scores [PMID:41332143]. In NALCN/CLIFAHDD reports described as CLIFAHDD/Baker-Gordon, neurodevelopmental regression was reported and was linked to onset of ataxia [PMID:40048676].

Behavioral, social, and psychiatric course. Behavioral morbidity may become more prominent with age. SYT1 reports include behavioral dysregulation and sleep disturbance at 12 years [PMID:41438914], poorly controlled agitation and mood swings despite antipsychotic medication [PMID:41438914], and ongoing learning/behavioral problems requiring special-needs education [PMID:38321119]. Older children and adolescents may develop social and emotional difficulties, including obsessions, anxieties, and mood disturbance [PMID:35101335]. Cross-sectional age-related data found increasing sleep difficulties and self-injurious behaviors from infancy into later childhood/adulthood [PMID:41332143], and older age was associated with more frequent or severe behavioral and emotional challenges on Developmental Behaviour Checklist scores [PMID:41332143].

Prognostic modifiers under study. Functional severity may relate to variant-specific synaptic effects: lower in vitro evoked exocytosis efficiency correlated with worse Vineland motor and communication scores in SYT1-associated disease [PMID:39481209]. Prognosis should remain individualized, because published SYT1/Baker-Gordon cohorts include both profound lifelong dependence [PMID:38283597] and milder developmental trajectories with measurable gains [PMID:32362337].

Epilepsy trajectory and treatment response

Overall epilepsy burden is variable and incompletely defined. In available SYT1/Baker-Gordon cohort data, epilepsy was reported in a minority of affected individuals, and the authors noted that additional cases are needed to clarify epilepsy risk [PMID:35101335]. The provided Baker-Gordon-specific reports do not establish a consistent neonatal-onset seizure pattern, recurrent epileptic spasms/West syndrome, or hypsarrhythmia as a defining trajectory [PMID:30107533, 35101335, 38321119, 41438914, 41332143].

EEG evolution may not parallel clinical seizure burden. In one SYT1 series with multiple EEGs across childhood, epileptiform features declined with age and some age-appropriate rhythms became observable by late childhood [PMID:30107533]. In contrast, an age-related SYT1 cohort found serial EEGs generally remained abnormal: 10 of 11 individuals remained overall abnormal and 1 became abnormal, with wakeful epileptiform activity emerging in 3 and persisting in 6 [PMID:41332143]. Staring episodes should be interpreted cautiously, as one child had staring spells at age 2 years without associated EEG abnormalities [PMID:38321119].

Treatment-response evidence is sparse. Case-level evidence indicates that seizures can be controllable: one 12-year-old with SYT1 disruption had seizures reported as well controlled on carbamazepine [PMID:41438914]. The provided Baker-Gordon-specific summaries do not provide systematic response data for valproate, ketogenic diet, corticosteroids, vigabatrin, vagus nerve stimulation, or other standardized antiseizure regimens [PMID:30107533, 35101335, 41438914, 41332143], so syndrome-specific treatment preferences or pharmacoresistance rates cannot yet be inferred.

Seizure control should be separated from developmental prognosis. Persistent neurodevelopmental impairment can remain prominent despite good seizure control: the carbamazepine-responsive SYT1 patient still had severe intellectual disability, severe speech impairment, behavioral dysregulation, and sleep disturbance at 12 years [PMID:41438914]. Broader SYT1 data show outcomes ranging from mild or moderate motor/communication delay in approximately one-third of cases to severe or profound developmental impairment [PMID:35101335], and older age was associated with lower adaptive functioning and more frequent or severe behavioral-emotional challenges in cross-sectional analysis [PMID:41332143].

Status epilepticus, recurrence, and seizure-related mortality are not well characterized. The provided SYT1/Baker-Gordon reports do not identify status epilepticus as a recurrent feature or document seizure-related death [PMID:30107533, 35101335, 41438914, 41332143]. Deaths were reported in an age-related SYT1 cohort, but the summarized data did not attribute them to epilepsy [PMID:41332143].

Neuromotor and structural complications

Motor tone and ambulation. Early hypotonia is a common neuromotor feature, including onset by about 3 months in a child with a multi-exon SYT1 deletion [PMID:42750022], while age-related data suggest hypotonia becomes less prevalent from infancy into later childhood/adulthood [PMID:41332143]. Motor outcome is heterogeneous: some individuals achieve delayed but meaningful ambulation, including walking after age 5 years in 2 older individuals [PMID:35101335], walking at 28 months with later running by 4 years in a milder D366E case [PMID:32362337], and preserved walking, running, and cycling at 10 years in another case [PMID:38321119]. More severe cases have persistent major motor dependence into adolescence/adulthood [PMID:38283597]. Lower evoked exocytosis efficiency in functional assays correlated with worse Vineland motor scores, supporting variant-level motor prognostic variability [PMID:39481209].

Gait decline and movement disorders. Longitudinal reports do not show a uniform progressive motor course: one series reported no developmental regression, including explicit absence of regression in the D366E case [PMID:32362337], whereas a broader cohort found late-childhood movement-disorder symptoms with relative decline in adaptive skills in some older individuals [PMID:35101335]. Movement disorders are age-associated in Baker-Gordon syndrome, with increasing frequency from infancy into later childhood/adulthood [PMID:41332143], but longer follow-up is needed to define emergence over time [PMID:30107533]. MRI did not show striatal or thalamic pathology or volume loss despite movement disorders in several patients [PMID:30107533], and functional measures correlated with adaptive motor impairment but not with the number of movement-disorder types [PMID:39481209]. The supplied SYT1 data do not establish spasticity, parkinsonism, hemiplegia, or a consistent ataxic syndrome as recurring Baker-Gordon complications.

Orthopedic and peripheral neuromotor complications. Clinically important musculoskeletal complications can emerge with age, including progressive lower-limb contractures and scoliosis in an adolescent D304G patient [PMID:30107533]. Scoliosis required spinal fusion with rod placement at 16 years in a D304G patient, after which the patient did not regain the ability to crawl or sit independently [PMID:32362337], and severe scoliosis was also reported in an adult patient followed to age 21 years [PMID:38058756]. Chronic motor neuropathy affecting all four limbs was highlighted in an I368T case as a previously unreported Baker-Gordon finding [PMID:38283597]. Hip dysplasia and fractures are not described as recurrent complications in the supplied SYT1-specific reports.

Head growth and structural neurologic progression. Neuroimaging in Baker-Gordon syndrome is heterogeneous and may be stable or resolving, but progressive structural change can occur. Serial imaging showed progressive atrophy in 5 individuals, including focal cerebellar atrophy, corpus callosal atrophy, and diffuse cortico-subcortical atrophy with secondary ventricular dilation [PMID:41332143]. No consistent striatal or thalamic volume loss was reported in one MRI series despite movement disorders [PMID:30107533]. Available SYT1 reports do not define persistent microcephaly or primary hydrocephalus as recurring structural complications; one multi-exon deletion case had normal birth parameters before later hypotonia and motor delay became apparent [PMID:42750022].

Medical morbidity and survival

Respiratory and sleep-related morbidity is a recurring prognostic issue, especially in infancy. Congenital central apnea in PURA syndrome generally improved during infancy and resolved by the first year, with no later breathing dysrhythmias or pulmonary complications reported in that cohort [PMID:29150892], and central sleep apnea requiring supplemental oxygen in infancy resolved by early childhood in reported SYT1 cases [PMID:30107533]. In contrast, CLIFAHDD/NALCN-related disease could show progressive apnea and respiratory insufficiency, including escalation from early apneas to ventilatory support by 12 months [PMID:35055596]; pyridostigmine was associated with fewer apnea episodes and no further urgent apnea-related hospitalizations, although concurrent CPAP and oxygen may have contributed [PMID:35055596]. Other respiratory presentations included obstructive apnea in a craniosynostosis–arachnodactyly–hernia syndrome [PMID:6182156], dyspnea with facial cyanosis in MAGEL2-positive infants [PMID:29581464], poor airway clearance requiring interventions in POLR2A-related disorder [PMID:33665635], and recurrent pneumonia or bronchiolitis hospitalizations in POLR2A-related disorder [PMID:33665635]. Sleep morbidity may persist or emerge with age, including increasing sleep difficulties in SYT1-associated disorder [PMID:41332143] and sleep disturbance in a 12-year-old with SYT1 disruption [PMID:41438914], whereas one SPATA5 family had normal sleep patterns [PMID:27683084].

Feeding, gastrointestinal, and growth complications can be clinically decisive. Dysphagia and reflux ranged from mild oropharyngeal dysmotility without dietary modification in an inherited PURA family [PMID:39131487] to severe SYT1-associated gastroesophageal reflux with food refusal in adolescence [PMID:30107533] and gastrostomy placement at 15 years for poor weight maintenance and feeding difficulty attributed to severe reflux [PMID:32362337]. Persistent dysphagia and feeding limitations were linked to marked underweight at age 18 in a SYT1 case [PMID:38283597], and severe neonatal feeding problems co-occurred with fatal breathing problems in a SYT1 C2A-domain variant [PMID:37930470]. Postoperative dysphagia and sialorrhea complicated treatment of posterior fossa ependymoma in POLR2A-related disease [PMID:35689525]. Vomiting was associated with recurrent respiratory infections in a PIGS case [PMID:32612635], constipation was a major persistent problem from infancy into adulthood in a SYNGAP1 case [PMID:28576131], and severe POLR2A-related infantile disease included failure to thrive [PMID:33665635]. Growth delay may improve in some individuals: childhood microcephaly and growth delay in a long-term SYNGAP1 case gradually normalized after puberty [PMID:28576131].

Other medical comorbidities include renal, sensory, endocrine, infectious, and pain-recognition issues. AMMECR1-related disease included persistent nephrocalcinosis with intermittent hypercalciuria [PMID:27811305], and POLR2A-related disease included recurrent urinary tract infections in one individual [PMID:33665635]. Progressive hearing deterioration requiring hearing aids occurred in AMMECR1-related disease [PMID:27811305], while hearing loss was part of the reported SPATA5 phenotype [PMID:27683084] and PIGS phenotype [PMID:32612635]. A SOX11 case had suspected hypogonadotropic hypogonadism with pubertal progression after pulsatile GnRH therapy, but the authors noted that off-treatment follow-up was needed to confirm persistence [PMID:42168980]. Severe pneumonia complicated by sepsis caused death in an OTUD5-related case, raising concern for possible immune vulnerability that requires validation [PMID:41838201]. Reduced pain responsiveness in SYNGAP1 may delay recognition of injuries or inflammation [PMID:30541864]. Additional non-epilepsy morbidity included mild idiopathic intracranial hypertension with bilateral papilledema that improved after lumbar punctures in a PUM1 case [PMID:29474920] and mild/subtle but reproducible elliptocytosis in AMMECR1-related disease [PMID:27811305].

Treatment-related morbidity was reported across several disorders. In adult PPP2R5D-related parkinsonism, levodopa-responsive disease was complicated by motor and nonmotor fluctuations [PMID:32743835], dopamine agonists caused impulse-control disorders in two cases [PMID:32743835], and dopamine-induced dyskinesias occurred in one case [PMID:32743835]. POLR2A-related cases had notable medication or anesthesia reactions, including levetiracetam-associated angioedema [PMID:33665635], adverse reaction to therapeutic botulinum toxin [PMID:33665635], fluoxetine-induced psychosis [PMID:33665635], and agitation with sedatives and general anesthesia [PMID:33665635]. A TBL1XR1 case developed abnormal liver function during treatment that normalized after liver-protection therapy [PMID:37171308].

Survival and causes of death were highly variable, from neonatal lethality to adult survival. Respiratory or feeding–respiratory mechanisms included a SYT1 newborn death at 57 days from severe feeding and breathing problems [PMID:37930470], a SYT1 cohort in which three individuals died after assessment at a mean age of 7.4 years and one newborn death was attributed to respiratory complications [PMID:41332143], and a PURA individual who died at 27 years after prolonged respiratory illness, attributed more to generalized neurologic decline than to a specific breathing-regulation disorder [PMID:29150892]. Aspiration pneumonia caused death in an adult SYNGAP1 case [PMID:30541864] and an adult PPP2R5D case [PMID:32743835], while pneumonia complicated by sepsis caused death at 1.5 years in an OTUD5-related case [PMID:41838201]. Cardiorespiratory/neonatal deaths included a MAGEL2-positive child who died of cardiovascular failure after dyspnea and cyanosis [PMID:29581464], another MAGEL2-positive infant who died one week after discharge despite ventilation-responsive dyspnea and nasal tube feeding [PMID:29581464], and a severe POLR2A infant with immature lungs, dilated cardiomyopathy, and failure to thrive who died during infancy [PMID:33665635]. Tumor-related mortality was reported in POLR2A-related disease, with death at 6 years after recurrent posterior fossa ependymoma despite multimodal therapy [PMID:35689525]. Seizure-asphyxia mortality was described in a PIGS case at 15 months [PMID:32612635], and severe PPP3CA regulatory-domain variants were described as having potentially fatal outcomes [PMID:39305655]. Early or premature death without a more specific mechanism was reported in WWOX-related disease, with all five patients dying before age 3 years [PMID:26345274], in severe KCNH5 pore-domain disease with childhood death [PMID:36307226], and in CLIFAHDD/NALCN pore-domain variants where premature death was noted [PMID:40048676].

Prognostic heterogeneity and modifiers

Variant- and mechanism-level prognosis is heterogeneous. SYT1-associated Baker-Gordon syndrome ranges from mild or moderate motor/communication delay in about one-third of reported individuals to severe or profound developmental impairment [PMID:35101335]. Variant-level observations suggest that D366E may be substantially milder than D304G and I368T [PMID:32362337], whereas I368T has been associated with severe impairment persisting into late adolescence [PMID:38283597] and a neonatal C2A-domain variant was lethal because of severe feeding and breathing problems [PMID:37930470]. Haploinsufficiency is also a plausible disease mechanism, but not necessarily a mild one: a child with SYT1 disruption had severe syndromic neurodevelopmental disorder at 12 years, although seizures were well controlled [PMID:41438914]. Available SYT1 reports provide limited intrafamilial prognostic data; in one isolated autosomal dominant, high-penetrance/low-fitness case, the authors estimated sibling recurrence risk as less than 0.021 [PMID:38283597].

Functional assay results may refine risk stratification. Lower in vitro evoked exocytosis efficiency correlated with worse Vineland motor and communication scores across corresponding SYT1 variants [PMID:39481209]. Motor adaptive function also correlated with initial exocytic rate and exocytic tau, linking slower or less efficient vesicle fusion to poorer motor function [PMID:39481209]. However, the same functional measures did not correlate significantly with number of movement-disorder types, cerebral visual impairment score, or self-injury score, so assay results should not be treated as global predictors of all clinical domains [PMID:39481209]. A de novo SYT1 missense variant that desynchronized neurotransmitter release was still associated with preserved gross motor abilities at age 10 years, illustrating that synaptic dysfunction does not by itself determine a uniformly severe motor trajectory [PMID:38321119].

Age strongly modifies the observed phenotype. Cross-sectional age-related data suggest decreasing hypotonia but increasing movement disorders, sleep difficulties, and self-injurious behaviors from infancy into later childhood or adulthood [PMID:41332143]. Older age was associated with lower adaptive functioning and more frequent or severe behavioral and emotional challenges [PMID:41332143]. Longitudinally, some individuals continue to gain skills: the D366E patient had no regression and gained walking, pincer grasp, self-feeding, running, and a few words by age 4 years [PMID:32362337], and two individuals older than 10 years learned to walk after age 5 years [PMID:35101335]. Conversely, some older children developed movement-disorder symptoms with relative adaptive decline [PMID:35101335], and serial imaging showed progressive atrophy in a minority despite heterogeneous MRI courses overall [PMID:41332143].

Seizure and treatment-response prognosis appears domain-specific and incompletely predictable. Epilepsy was reported in a minority of the broader SYT1 cohort, and the authors cautioned that additional cases are needed to clarify epilepsy risk [PMID:35101335]. Individual responses vary: seizures were well controlled with carbamazepine in one severe haploinsufficiency case [PMID:41438914], whereas serial EEGs in another cohort generally remained abnormal, with wakeful epileptiform activity emerging or persisting in several individuals [PMID:41332143]. Staring episodes in one child were not associated with EEG abnormalities, underscoring the need to distinguish behavioral events from epilepsy when counseling families [PMID:38321119]. Behavioral symptoms may be less treatment-responsive than seizures in some patients, as agitation and mood swings were poorly controlled despite antipsychotic medications in one case [PMID:41438914].

Prognostic counseling should remain cautious. The developmental trajectory remains uncertain because many reported individuals are young and long-term data are limited [PMID:35101335]. Single-case phenotype expansions should be interpreted carefully, including chronic four-limb motor neuropathy reported as a previously undescribed finding in one late-adolescent I368T case [PMID:38283597]. Mortality has been reported, including neonatal death from respiratory complications in a severe C2A-domain case [PMID:37930470] and three deaths after assessment in an age-related cohort with mean age at death 7.4 years [PMID:41332143], but current data are insufficient to quantify variant-specific survival risk reliably.

Diagnostic Evaluation

When to suspect SYT1-associated Baker-Gordon syndrome

Suspect SYT1-associated Baker-Gordon syndrome in an infant or child with neonatal or early hypotonia and global developmental delay, especially when accompanied by marked speech/communication and adaptive motor impairment [PMID:35101335, 39481209]. The full pattern does not need to be present: one expanded cohort recommended considering the diagnosis when neonatal hypotonia, developmental delay, abnormal eye physiology, movement disorder, and EEG abnormalities occur in any combination [PMID:35101335].

Movement and visual features should further lower the threshold for testing. Movement disorder is part of the proposed clinical suspicion pattern [PMID:35101335], and an age-related cohort found strong concordance between movement disorders and epileptiform EEG activity [PMID:41332143]. Abnormal eye physiology is also part of the recurring phenotype [PMID:35101335]; paired-pulse visual evoked potential abnormalities were reported in an early affected individual [PMID:25705886], and later studies incorporated cerebral visual impairment scales to characterize visual function [PMID:39481209, 41332143].

Behavioral and autism-related features are also relevant and should be assessed systematically rather than inferred only from clinical observation. Cohorts used the Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scales/Vineland-3 for adaptive communication and motor function [PMID:35101335, 39481209, 41332143], the Developmental Behavior Checklist for emotional/behavioral and self-injury features [PMID:35101335, 39481209, 41332143], the Social Responsiveness Scale-2 for autism-related social features [PMID:35101335, 41332143], and the Repetitive Behavior Questionnaire for repetitive behaviors [PMID:41332143].

EEG abnormalities are frequent enough that an abnormal EEG in this neurodevelopmental context should prompt consideration of SYT1-associated disease, even when clinical seizures are not evident. Clinical EEG was abnormal in all 11 individuals in one case series [PMID:30107533], in 94% of tested individuals in an expanded cohort [PMID:35101335], and in 25/27 individuals with classifiable EEG data in an age-related cohort [PMID:41332143]. Epileptiform activity may occur without overt seizures or behavioral seizure manifestations [PMID:30107533, 41332143], and some EEG features vary with age [PMID:30107533], supporting repeat or age-contextualized interpretation when suspicion remains high.

Because the presentation can be age-dependent and multidomain, testing is most strongly indicated when early hypotonia/developmental delay is joined by any combination of severe communication impairment, movement disorder, abnormal visual physiology or cerebral visual impairment, behavioral/autism-related features, and recurrent EEG abnormalities [PMID:35101335, 39481209, 41332143]. Standardized adaptive, behavioral, autism, and visual-function measures can help document the recognizable pattern and severity profile before or alongside genomic testing [PMID:35101335, 39481209, 41332143].

Molecular confirmation with SYT1 sequencing

Preferred sequencing strategy. Molecular confirmation of Baker-Gordon syndrome is usually achieved by genome-wide sequencing that can detect heterozygous SYT1 single-nucleotide variants and small indels, especially trio exome sequencing or trio genome sequencing when available [PMID:25705886, 30107533, 41332143]. Reported diagnoses have also been made by clinical or research exome sequencing performed as trio or solo testing, with de novo status subsequently confirmed by the clinical center [PMID:35101335, 39481209]. Orthogonal confirmation and segregation testing are important: published cases used Sanger confirmation of the proband variant and parental samples to establish de novo occurrence [PMID:30107533, 32362337, 37930470], repeat exome analysis with an independent pull-down method as confirmation [PMID:30107533], or later parental genotyping to support de novo status [PMID:38283597].

Variant interpretation. Candidate SYT1 variants should be interpreted using ACMG/AMP or related best-practice frameworks [PMID:35101335, 38283597], integrating confirmed de novo status [PMID:30107533, 37930470, 38321119, 41332143], absence from population databases such as gnomAD [PMID:30107533, 38321119], evolutionary conservation of the altered residue [PMID:30107533, 38321119], and consistency with the patient’s neurodevelopmental phenotype [PMID:30107533, 35101335]. Most reported pathogenic variants cluster in the C2B domain [PMID:30107533], and de novo missense variants in C2B calcium-binding loops warrant particular scrutiny [PMID:35101335]. Variants affecting other highly conserved SYT1 C2-domain residues should also be considered [PMID:35101335], including rare reports outside C2B such as a de novo C2A-domain p.V184A variant [PMID:37930470]. Localization to functionally relevant regions, such as the C2B-domain/SNARE-interface region for p.Pro401Leu, has been used as supporting evidence for pathogenicity [PMID:38321119].

Excluding a better alternative diagnosis. Molecular confirmation should include review of the sequencing result for competing diagnoses; one cohort required no alternative candidate variant more likely to explain the neurodevelopmental presentation [PMID:41332143], and one WES-confirmed p.Ile368Thr case reported no additional reportable variant [PMID:38283597]. Many individuals had extensive nondiagnostic prior evaluations before SYT1 was identified, including karyotype, Fragile X testing, chromosomal microarray or SNP array, Angelman/Rett or other targeted testing, and broad metabolic studies [PMID:32362337, 38321119, 38283597]. Normal or nonspecific ancillary findings should not preclude SYT1 testing: brain MRI was normal in multiple molecularly confirmed individuals [PMID:25705886, 30107533, 32362337, 38283597], and EEG may be abnormal without overt seizures but is not required for sequence confirmation [PMID:30107533, 32362337, 38321119].

Functional evidence. Functional exocytosis assays can provide supportive pathogenicity evidence for uncertain SYT1 missense variants but are not routine first-line diagnostic tests. Research studies have used cultured hippocampal neurons expressing SYT1-pHluorin to assess protein expression, presynaptic localization, vesicular targeting, recycling and readily releasable vesicle pools, and evoked exocytosis kinetics [PMID:39481209]. These assays have been proposed to help establish pathogenicity and may correlate with severity of adaptive motor and communication impairment [PMID:39481209], while mechanistic studies of specific de novo variants have shown altered synaptic vesicle cycling or desynchronized neurotransmitter release [PMID:25705886, 38321119].

SYT1 dosage and structural variant testing

Dosage-first interpretation. Evaluation should distinguish an isolated SYT1 lesion from a broader chromosome 12q21 deletion. Array CGH has identified overlapping 12q21 deletions that include SYT1 and PPP1R12A [PMID:32633079], whereas other reported Baker-Gordon–relevant mechanisms include an intragenic SYT1 exon 6–8 deletion [PMID:42750022], an intronic SYT1-disrupting rearrangement [PMID:41438914], and a regulatory rearrangement with reduced SYT1 expression [PMID:37916192]. When a 12q21 deletion is detected, the report should document size, breakpoints when available, and all included genes, because deletion of neighboring genes should be considered when the phenotype is broader than expected for isolated SYT1 disruption [PMID:32633079, 42750022].

Chromosomal microarray / array CGH. CMA or array CGH is useful for detecting larger 12q21 copy-number losses, including prenatal or postnatal deletions used to define the SYT1-containing critical region [PMID:32633079]. Its key limitation is that it can be normal in pathogenic balanced or copy-neutral SYT1 rearrangements: one patient had a G-banded t(5;12) with no clinically relevant CNV on chromosomal microarray, and the SYT1-disrupting mechanism was resolved only by optical genome mapping and FISH [PMID:41438914]. A normal conventional karyotype also does not exclude Baker-Gordon–like disease, as earlier pattern-recognized affected children had normal karyotyping [PMID:6182156].

Exome-based exon-level CNV analysis. Clinical exome sequencing should be reviewed for exon-level SYT1 dosage, not only sequence variants, because read-depth CNV calling detected reduced normalized depth across SYT1 exons 6–8 consistent with a heterozygous multi-exon deletion [PMID:42750022]. In that report, deletion and duplication thresholds were copy-number ratios below 0.7 and above 1.3 [PMID:42750022]. Orthogonal confirmation is important: exon-specific qPCR for SYT1 exons 6, 7, and 8 using ALB as the reference locus confirmed approximately 50% dosage reduction in the proband [PMID:42750022], and parental qPCR showed normal diploid dosage, establishing de novo occurrence [PMID:42750022].

Balanced and complex structural rearrangements. Conventional GTG- or G-banded karyotyping can still be diagnostically important when a balanced translocation is visible, including t(7;12)(q21.3;q21.2) in a patient with Baker-Gordon–like features [PMID:37916192] and 46,XX,t(5;12)(q31;q21) in a patient later shown to have SYT1 disruption [PMID:41438914]. However, karyotype alone may not define the pathogenic architecture: whole-genome sequencing mapped a complex translocation/inversion/reorganization affecting SYT1 in the t(7;12) case [PMID:37916192], and PCR followed by Sanger sequencing confirmed the WGS-defined breakpoints [PMID:37916192]. Optical genome mapping further illustrates the value of genome-scale structural testing, revealing a four-breakpoint rearrangement with a paracentric inversion and localizing the SYT1-disrupting breakpoint to intron 9 of NM_005639.3 [PMID:41438914].

Breakpoint confirmation and functional support. FISH can confirm rearrangement structure when targeted probes are selected appropriately: BAC clones flanking SYT1 demonstrated a signal gap on the derivative chromosome consistent with the paracentric inversion in the OGM-resolved case [PMID:41438914]. For suspected regulatory or noncoding disruption, expression studies may provide functional evidence: PBMC RNA comparative ΔΔCt analysis showed SYT1 expression reduced to 0.3 of the control mean in the patient with the complex t(7;12) rearrangement, supporting pathogenicity of the regulatory SYT1 disruption [PMID:37916192]. Thus, when CMA or exome sequencing is unrevealing but the phenotype remains suggestive, structural breakpoint mapping and, when feasible, SYT1 expression testing can clarify cryptic or copy-neutral mechanisms [PMID:37916192, 41438914].

Neurophysiologic testing

EEG is a supportive test, not a standalone diagnostic test. In Baker-Gordon syndrome, EEG findings can strengthen clinical suspicion when paired with a compatible neurodevelopmental phenotype, but neurophysiology should not be considered diagnostic without a compatible pathogenic or likely pathogenic SYT1 molecular finding [PMID:30107533, 35101335, 41332143]. Across cohorts, EEG abnormalities were frequent: abnormal in all 11 individuals in one case series [PMID:30107533], present in 94% of tested individuals in an expanded cohort [PMID:35101335], and present in 25/27 individuals with classifiable EEG data in an age-related study [PMID:41332143].

Typical EEG background abnormalities include symmetrical bursts of high-amplitude, low-frequency synchronous activity [PMID:30107533], low-frequency background oscillations or generalized/background slowing [PMID:35101335], abnormal posterior background rhythms and bursts of synchronous high-voltage polymorphic slow waves during wakefulness [PMID:41332143], and diffuse/background slowing with high-amplitude slow waves [PMID:32362337]. An early molecularly characterized case also showed extensive neurophysiologic abnormalities with low-frequency oscillatory bursts [PMID:25705886].

Epileptiform activity may be present even without obvious clinical seizures. In one cohort, 5 individuals without overt seizures had epileptiform EEG abnormalities, including multifocal spikes, isolated spike bursts, isolated sharp waves, and generalized spike-wave discharges, often with parietal-occipital maxima [PMID:30107533]. In a larger age-related cohort, epileptiform activity was reported in 19/27 individuals during wakefulness and in 9/13 with sleep EEG data, often focal or multifocal with bilateral posterior predominance and frequently without behavioral manifestations of seizure [PMID:41332143]. Ictal features have also been reported when captured [PMID:35101335]. Conversely, a normal EEG was reported in a patient evaluated for staring episodes [PMID:38321119], and EEG performed during seizure evaluation was normal in another individual with a SYT1-disrupting chromosomal rearrangement [PMID:41438914].

Visual evoked potentials may be useful when visual pathway dysfunction is suspected. Reported abnormalities include enhanced paired-pulse depression on paired-pulse visual evoked potential testing [PMID:25705886] and increased or prolonged latency with reduced amplitude in individuals with D366E and I368T variants [PMID:32362337]. These findings support targeted VEP use in patients with suspected cortical or visual pathway involvement rather than routine use in all cases.

Peripheral neurophysiology is selective. Electroneuromyography was abnormal in one reported individual, showing chronic motor neuropathy in all four limbs [PMID:38283597]. EMG or electroneuromyography is therefore best reserved for patients with peripheral neuromotor signs that require clarification, rather than as a routine diagnostic test for SYT1-related disease [PMID:38283597].

Neuroimaging and ancillary exclusion tests

Brain MRI is helpful for documenting structural comorbidity and excluding alternative diagnoses, but it is often normal or nonspecific in SYT1-related Baker-Gordon syndrome. Normal structural MRI has been reported despite severe neurodevelopmental and movement manifestations [PMID:25705886], in 7/11 individuals in one case series [PMID:30107533], in all 3 patients in another molecular series [PMID:32362337], after seizure evaluation in a patient with a SYT1-disrupting rearrangement [PMID:41438914], and in 17/31 individuals with available MRI data in an age-related cohort [PMID:41332143]. MRI abnormalities were present in about one-third of individuals with available imaging in one expanded cohort [PMID:35101335] and in 14/31 individuals in the age-related cohort [PMID:41332143]. Magnetic resonance spectroscopy, when obtained, may also be unrevealing; it was normal in 2 patients in one case series [PMID:30107533].

Reported MRI findings span developmental, malformative, and acquired-appearing changes rather than a single diagnostic pattern. Findings have included delayed maturation [PMID:30107533], transient or uncertain white-matter/CSF-space abnormalities [PMID:30107533], infant choroid plexus hemorrhage with later normal MRI [PMID:30107533], irregular skull morphology [PMID:41332143], delayed or immature myelination [PMID:41332143], cysts or masses [PMID:41332143], hypoplasia [PMID:41332143], heterotopia [PMID:41332143], white-matter lesions or abnormalities [PMID:41332143], cortical or subcortical atrophy [PMID:41332143], and hemorrhagic lesions [PMID:41332143]. Delayed myelination was mainly reported in infancy and typically resolved by childhood [PMID:41332143]. A de novo multi-exon SYT1 deletion case had a simplified frontal and parietal gyral pattern on brain MRI [PMID:42750022].

Ancillary testing should be guided by the presenting features and differential diagnosis, but normal results do not exclude SYT1-related disease. Ophthalmologic evaluation and ECG were unremarkable in one patient later diagnosed with a de novo SYT1 p.Ile368Thr variant [PMID:38283597], and endocrine evaluation was normal in a patient evaluated for unexplained tall stature before identification of a de novo SYT1 p.Pro401Leu variant [PMID:38321119]. Urine testing may be normal or nonspecific: one case had only a slight increase in urine glycine [PMID:38283597], while urinary organic acids and acylcarnitine profile were normal in a patient with a SYT1-disrupting chromosomal rearrangement [PMID:41438914].

Metabolic and syndromic evaluations are frequently nondiagnostic before SYT1 is identified. Reported normal or unrevealing studies have included serum amino acids, urine organic acids, acyl-carnitine testing, very-long-chain fatty acids, creatine kinase, and mitochondrial genome sequencing [PMID:32362337]. Broader prior evaluations in individual cases included karyotype or high-resolution chromosomes, Fragile X DNA analysis, Angelman testing or methylation, MECP2 deletion/duplication testing, Joubert panel testing, glycosylation studies, creatine/guanidinoacetate studies, purine/pyrimidine panels, gangliosidosis testing, phytanic acids, mucopolysaccharidosis testing, neuronal ceroid lipofuscinosis testing, TSH, and hearing/auditory brainstem response testing, all before SYT1 diagnosis by exome sequencing [PMID:32362337].

Conventional cytogenetic and chromosomal tests may be useful for excluding common alternatives but can miss SYT1-related mechanisms. Normal karyotype, normal FMR1 CGG repeat length, and normal SNP array were reported before diagnosis of a de novo SYT1 missense variant [PMID:38321119]; FISH for Smith-Magenis syndrome, FMR1 expansion testing, and chromosomal microarray were also normal before exome-based diagnosis in another patient [PMID:38283597]. Conversely, G-banded karyotype identified a balanced translocation in one patient, while chromosomal microarray did not detect a clinically relevant copy-number variant; optical genome mapping and FISH then localized a SYT1-disrupting breakpoint [PMID:41438914]. Exome sequencing with read-depth exon-level CNV analysis can identify clinically relevant intragenic SYT1 deletions, as shown by detection and qPCR confirmation of a de novo deletion of exons 6–8 [PMID:42750022].

Differential Diagnosis

Syndromic neurodevelopmental phenocopies

Nonspecific neurodevelopmental labels. Baker-Gordon syndrome may enter the differential as unexplained global developmental delay after routine laboratory, metabolic, EEG, ophthalmologic, and hearing evaluations are unrevealing [PMID:42750022], or as pervasive developmental disorder/autistic features before SYT1 diagnosis [PMID:38321119]. Individually nonspecific findings can obscure recognition, but the combination of neonatal hypotonia, developmental delay, severe motor and communication impairment, abnormal eye physiology, movement disorder, behavioral dysregulation, and EEG abnormalities should raise suspicion for SYT1-associated Baker-Gordon syndrome [PMID:35101335]. In comparative data, SYT1-affected individuals showed more severe motor and communication impairment than other monogenic intellectual disability groups, with relatively elevated self-injury, rapid mood changes, and repetitive movements [PMID:35101335]. Other neurodevelopmental disorders can occupy similar initial diagnostic space: TBL1XR1-related disease included poor eye contact and poor response to name-calling that could not be clearly separated from developmental delay versus autistic tendencies [PMID:37171308], and inherited PURA-related disease could resemble an isolated primary speech disorder with borderline intellect when seizures, hearing loss, MRI abnormalities, and moderate-to-severe intellectual disability were absent [PMID:39131487].

Angelman-like presentations. SYNGAP1 is an important Angelman-like phenocopy: in one SYNGAP1 loss-of-function cohort, 8 of 10 individuals had previously been investigated for Angelman syndrome, leading the authors to recommend SYNGAP1 in the differential for Angelman or Angelman-like presentations [PMID:26079862]; another SYNGAP1 case had Angelman syndrome considered before exome sequencing [PMID:28576131]. Angelman syndrome was also part of the nondiagnostic workup in SYT1-associated disease, including one patient tested for Fragile X syndrome, Angelman syndrome, lysosomal storage disorders, and metabolic disorders before the de novo SYT1 variant was found [PMID:32362337], and another tested for Angelman syndrome along with Rett/MECP2, Joubert syndrome, glycosylation, mitochondrial, creatine/GAA, purine/pyrimidine, and metabolic disorders before SYT1 diagnosis [PMID:32362337]. Prader-Willi/Angelman-region methylation testing can also arise in broader developmental-delay evaluations; in AMMECR1-related disease, SNRPN exon 1 methylation was normal after testing prompted by neonatal hypotonia and poor feeding [PMID:27811305].

Rett-like presentations. Rett-like categorization should prompt attention to the gene-level context rather than reliance on stereotypies alone. HNRNPU-related disease can include stereotypic hand movements and abnormal breathing episodes resembling Rett syndrome [PMID:29858110], and HNRNPU loss-of-function phenotypes overlap with clinical features seen in contiguous 1q43-q44 deletion syndrome [PMID:28815871]. STXBP1-related disorders may be labeled atypical Rett syndrome, with 13 individuals in a 534-person cohort assigned that category [PMID:35190816]. GABBR2-related disease can include Rett-like features [PMID:35414446], while FOXG1 syndrome may present with infantile hypotonia and postnatal microcephaly without symptoms suggestive of typical Rett syndrome at presentation [PMID:34964776]. In SYT1-associated disease, Rett syndrome/MECP2 deletion-duplication was specifically evaluated and was nondiagnostic in at least one patient before SYT1 diagnosis [PMID:32362337].

Movement, startle, and EEG phenocopies. Motor and paroxysmal phenomena may lead to cerebral palsy or seizure-adjacent labels. In PURA syndrome, gait abnormalities with increased tone or ataxia frequently led to a cerebral palsy diagnosis [PMID:29150892], and exaggerated startle could mimic seizures despite no epileptiform EEG correlate in 5 individuals [PMID:29150892]. In SYT1-associated Baker-Gordon syndrome, marked EEG abnormalities can occur without clinical seizures, which may help separate the syndrome from epileptic encephalopathy labels during evaluation of developmental delay with abnormal EEG [PMID:30107533]. Repetitive stereotypies in SYT1-associated disease may be difficult to distinguish from involuntary movements and may require repeated observations across settings and times of day [PMID:30107533], and one affected individual was described as having dystonia/dyskinetic cerebral palsy [PMID:30107533].

Fragile X, Smith-Magenis, chromosomal, and broader syndromic workups. Baker-Gordon syndrome may be considered only after common genetic mimics are excluded: one SYT1 patient had normal FMR1 repeat testing, karyotype, and SNP array before molecular diagnosis [PMID:38321119], and another had normal Smith-Magenis FISH and normal FMR1 expansion PCR during a long diagnostic journey attributed to phenotypic overlap among neurodevelopmental disorders [PMID:38283597]. Fragile X can also coexist with a second diagnosis; in one girl with established Fragile X syndrome, the phenotype was considered incompletely explained by Fragile X alone, prompting identification of PPP2R5D-related disorder after multiple other gene tests were negative [PMID:34946857]. Normal brain MRI and absent metabolic abnormalities in SYT1-associated cases can help redirect the workup away from structural malformation or metabolic etiologies despite profound developmental impairment [PMID:32362337].

Epileptic encephalopathy and seizure mimics

Abnormal EEG without overt seizures. In Baker-Gordon syndrome/SYT1-associated neurodevelopmental disorder, marked EEG abnormalities may occur without clinical seizures [PMID:30107533], and behavioral manifestations suggesting seizures were generally not observed during abnormal EEG patterns despite frequent epileptiform abnormalities [PMID:41332143]. This distinction is important when evaluating developmental delay with an abnormal EEG, because an epileptiform interictal record alone should not be equated with a developmental and epileptic encephalopathy in this condition [PMID:30107533, 41332143]. Diagnostic workups may otherwise be unrevealing: one child was considered to have global developmental delay of unknown etiology after routine laboratory, metabolic, EEG, ophthalmologic, and hearing evaluations did not identify an alternative cause [PMID:42750022], and earlier SYT1 cases had normal or nondiagnostic metabolic testing and normal brain MRI despite profound developmental impairment [PMID:32362337].

Paroxysmal seizure mimics. Repetitive stereotypies in SYT1-associated disease can be difficult to distinguish from involuntary or paroxysmal events, and the authors emphasized repeated observations across settings and times of day [PMID:30107533]. Exaggerated startle in PURA syndrome may mimic seizures but lacked an epileptiform EEG correlate in 5 individuals [PMID:29150892]; sleep-related dyskinesias in ADCY5-associated disease raised concern for seizures but video-EEG telemetry showed no epileptiform activity [PMID:27061943]; early apnea attacks in a PURA microdeletion case were not attributed to epilepsy because EEG showed no epileptic discharge [PMID:29619234]; and early apnea episodes in CLIFAHDD syndrome were initially treated as breath-holding spells before the genetic diagnosis [PMID:35055596]. These examples support careful event capture with video-EEG before labeling startle, apnea, dyskinesia, or breath-holding-like spells as epileptic.

Early-life epileptic encephalopathy differentials. SCN2A-related epilepsy can be diagnosed clinically as Ohtahara syndrome, West syndrome, Dravet syndrome, febrile seizures plus, benign familial infantile epilepsy, or benign familial neonatal-infantile epilepsy [PMID:29996185]. STXBP1-related disorders may present under West syndrome, Ohtahara syndrome, early-onset epileptic encephalopathy, other developmental and epileptic encephalopathy, nonspecific neurodevelopmental disorder, or atypical Rett labels [PMID:35190816]; one series included a child diagnosed with early infantile myoclonic encephalopathy and later Lennox-Gastaut-like features, although repeated EEGs did not show typical Lennox-Gastaut features [PMID:37908909]. Biallelic SLC12A5 was identified in an EIMFS cohort after exclusion of other known EIMFS genes [PMID:27436767], and one individual was classified as intractable epilepsy rather than definite EIMFS because ictal EEGs were unavailable, although parent-observed multifocal migrating seizures in infancy supported possible EIMFS [PMID:27436767]. PPP3CA-related disease includes nonsyndromic early-onset epileptic encephalopathy associated with catalytic-domain or truncating variants [PMID:29432562], and a patient with PPP3CA-related developmental and epileptic encephalopathy was initially diagnosed with West syndrome/infile spasms syndrome because of infantile spasms and modified hypsarrhythmia [PMID:36158964]. PIGS-related GPI deficiency should be considered when infantile spasms or intractable seizures occur with severe global developmental delay, hearing loss, visual impairment, hypotonia, feeding difficulty, and multiple external abnormalities [PMID:32612635].

Generalized epilepsy phenotypes overlapping DEE. SYNGAP1 encephalopathy can resemble epilepsy with eyelid myoclonia with absences and epilepsy with myoclonic-atonic seizures, with 35% of one cohort showing overlapping features of both syndromes [PMID:30541864]. Atypical absences in SYNGAP1 may suggest Lennox-Gastaut syndrome, but tonic seizures and generalized paroxysmal fast activity were generally absent, apart from one patient with nocturnal tonic seizures [PMID:30541864]. West syndrome was rare but reported in one SYNGAP1 patient with infantile spasms [PMID:30541864], and Angelman syndrome or Angelman-like presentations were common prior considerations in SYNGAP1-related disease, with 8/10 individuals previously investigated for Angelman syndrome [PMID:26079862]. Focal epilepsy with or without intellectual disability was reported in two individuals with SYNGAP1 variants of uncertain significance and lower conservation/pathogenicity scores, and was considered phenotypically distinct from the main SYNGAP1 encephalopathy cohort [PMID:30541864].

Excluding acquired, treatable, and gene-specific alternatives. In refractory infantile seizures, infection, immune encephalitis, hypoxic-ischemic injury, hypoglycemia, metabolic disease, and vitamin/cofactor-responsive epileptic encephalopathies may enter the differential: CSF excluded infection in most fever-associated STXBP1 cases [PMID:36440324], anti-MOG encephalitis co-occurred in one patient before persistent seizures led to STXBP1 diagnosis [PMID:36440324], seizures persisted despite correction of hypoglycemia in one STXBP1 case [PMID:37908909], and metabolic workup or vitamin/cofactor trials did not support treatable epileptic encephalopathies in affected STXBP1 patients [PMID:37908909]. PPP3CA genetic testing cohorts excluded epilepsy with acquired factors such as perinatal brain injury, traumatic brain injury, and central nervous system infections [PMID:40548073]. ACTL6B-related disease is another gene-defined comparator in which seizure burden helps stratify phenotypes: seizures occurred in 70/75 recessive cases but only 2/26 dominant cases [PMID:39275948]. In SYT1 evaluations, exclusion of an alternative pathogenic variant or unsupported variant class remains important when interpreting candidate findings [PMID:41332143].

Cerebral palsy and movement-disorder phenocopies

Cerebral-palsy labels are common in monogenic movement disorders. ADCY5-associated disease is a key phenocopy: most patients in one series were initially diagnosed with dyskinetic cerebral palsy [PMID:27061943], and an SGCE-negative family labeled as myoclonus-dystonia was later found to have ADCY5 p.R418G [PMID:27061943]. In such patients, axial hypotonia and facial dyskinesia were highlighted as clues favoring ADCY5-associated disease over SGCE-related myoclonus-dystonia [PMID:27061943]. PURA syndrome may also be labeled as cerebral palsy when gait abnormality is accompanied by increased tone or ataxia [PMID:29150892]. SYT1-associated neurodevelopmental disorder can similarly resemble dyskinetic cerebral palsy, with one case described as dystonia/dyskinetic cerebral palsy [PMID:30107533].

Separating stereotypies from involuntary movements may require longitudinal observation. In SYT1-associated disease, repetitive stereotypies can be difficult to distinguish from true involuntary movements [PMID:30107533], and the authors emphasized repeated observations across settings and times of day [PMID:30107533]. A broader SYT1 cohort found a discriminating profile of severe motor and communication impairment [PMID:35101335], with repetitive movements, self-injury, and rapid mood changes more elevated than in comparison monogenic intellectual-disability disorders [PMID:35101335]. The combination of neonatal hypotonia, developmental delay, abnormal eye physiology, and movement disorder should raise suspicion for SYT1-associated neurodevelopmental disorder rather than an acquired cerebral-palsy diagnosis [PMID:35101335].

Paroxysmal and episodic movement disorders broaden the differential. GABBR2-related disease may present with paroxysmal limb dystonias and Rett-like features, creating overlap with Rett-spectrum presentations [PMID:35414446]. Among NALCN/UNC80 channelosome disorders, episodic ataxia and paroxysmal dystonia were reported only in CLIFAHDD [PMID:40048676], whereas failure to thrive, central sleep apnea, and epilepsy were more frequent in loss-of-function NALCN/UNC80 disorders [PMID:40048676]. Heterozygous NALCN-related disease should also be considered in syndromic congenital ataxia, including presentations with progressive cerebellar atrophy [PMID:39914470].

Ataxia mimics should be separated by developmental history. PUM1 haploinsufficiency causing PADDAS/Baker-Gordon–spectrum developmental disease is clinically distinct from the reported PUM1 p.Thr1035Ser adult-onset cerebellar ataxia family, in which cerebellar ataxia occurred without developmental delay, intellectual disability, or seizures [PMID:29474920]. In a congenital presentation, an additional de novo XPR1 nonsense variant was considered less consistent with primary familial brain calcification, although later emergence of that disorder could not be excluded [PMID:29474920]. ATN1-related CHEDDA syndrome should be distinguished from dentatorubral-pallidoluysian atrophy because CHEDDA is associated with ATN1 exon 7 variants rather than ATN1 exon 5 expansion variants [PMID:34212383].

ACTL6B disease illustrates why inheritance pattern matters in dyskinetic-spastic neurodevelopmental presentations. Recessive ACTL6B-related developmental and epileptic-dyskinetic encephalopathy had a much higher seizure burden than dominant ACTL6B-related disease, with seizures in 70/75 recessive cases versus 2/26 dominant cases [PMID:39275948]. Motor severity also differed: hypertonia/spasticity occurred in 76% of recessive cases versus 25% of dominant cases [PMID:39275948], and dystonia occurred in 55% of recessive cases versus 27% of dominant cases [PMID:39275948]. Thus, prominent dystonia or spasticity with a heavy epilepsy burden should push the differential toward recessive ACTL6B disease rather than the milder dominant ACTL6B presentation [PMID:39275948].

Dysmorphic and multisystem phenotypes

Coffin-Siris/KBG-like dysmorphism is a common entry point: SOX11-related disease may present as a mild Coffin-Siris phenotype overlapping BAF-complex Coffin-Siris syndromes [PMID:24886874], and ANKRD11-related KBG syndrome may initially be labeled Coffin-Siris-like when coarse face, thick lower lip, wide mouth, long eyelashes, ear anomalies, and delayed dentition are prominent [PMID:28250421]. Useful discriminators include macrodontia as a frequent KBG feature [PMID:28250421] and fifth-finger brachydactyly/clinodactyly without fifth-finger or nail hypoplasia in ANKRD11-related cases [PMID:28250421], whereas absent or hypoplastic fifth finger/nail, sparse hair, feeding difficulty, hypotonia, joint laxity, body hypertrichosis, and recurrent infections were absent in the Coffin-Siris-like ANKRD11 cases [PMID:28250421]. Lack of a consistent gestalt should not be overinterpreted: PP2A-B56-related overgrowth may lack distinctive facial features [PMID:25972378], and ACTL6B-related dominant and recessive neurodevelopmental disorders had recurrent facial findings but no recognizable facial gestalt [PMID:39275948].

Overgrowth, macrocephaly, or tall stature should prompt parallel evaluation for Sotos-spectrum and PP2A-related disorders. PPP2R5D-related disease may be suspected after Sotos-like overgrowth, but one p.E200K case had negative NSD1 and NFIX testing before exome diagnosis [PMID:32743835]; another PPP2R5D evaluation followed negative testing of multiple overgrowth or connective-tissue genes, including NFIX, EZH2, NSD1, SETD2, and COL6A1–COL6A3 [PMID:34946857]. Incidental or secondary findings require phenotype matching: de novo SOS1, DCAF7, or CACNA1H variants were not considered causal in PPP2R5D cases because gene function and phenotype did not fit [PMID:26576547], and a PARK7 frameshift was deprioritized when present in an unaffected sister while PPP2R5D p.E200K was proband-specific [PMID:32743835]. Noonan syndrome may enter the differential when short stature, hypertelorism, webbed neck, cryptorchidism, and congenital heart disease coexist, but ANKRD11 diagnosis followed negative Noonan-gene testing in such a case [PMID:28250421]; endocrine causes of tall stature were also considered in a SYT1/Baker-Gordon case and endocrine function was normal [PMID:38321119].

Broader multisystem findings may redirect the differential. AMMECR1-related disease can combine developmental delay, midface hypoplasia, nephrocalcinosis, and elliptocytosis [PMID:27811305], leading to consideration of OCRL1-related Lowe/Dent disease, Stickler syndrome, chondrodysplasia punctata, Prader-Willi/Angelman-region disorders, midline defects, KDM5C-related intellectual disability, or MAGEC1 before targeted exclusions [PMID:27811305]. POLR2A-related disease is heterogeneous and multisystemic; mitochondrial disease workup may be triggered by findings such as skeletal-muscle complex IV deficiency, although mtDNA testing was negative in the reported individual [PMID:33665635]. GPI-anchor deficiency should be considered when multiple external anomalies accompany severe global developmental delay, intractable seizures, hearing loss, visual impairment, hypotonia, and feeding difficulty [PMID:32612635]. HNRNPU-related disease overlaps the 1q43-q44 deletion phenotype [PMID:28815871], and CHEDDA syndrome is distinguished from dentatorubral-pallidoluysian atrophy by ATN1 exon 7 variants rather than ATN1 exon 5 expansion variants [PMID:34212383].

Congenital respiratory, neuromuscular, and channelopathy presentations overlap substantially with neonatal syndromic differentials. Baker-Gordon syndrome or NALCN-related disease can be an alternative diagnosis in neonatal Crisponi/cold-induced sweating syndrome-like presentations after negative testing of known CS/CISS genes [PMID:30859550]. CLIFAHDD may initially mimic congenital myasthenic syndrome because of hypotonia, distal arthrogryposis, central apnea, and respiratory insufficiency [PMID:35055596], with RAPSN and CHAT considered clinically [PMID:35055596], and SMA or familial dysautonomia excluded by SMN1 MLPA and ELP1 sequencing [PMID:35055596]. In NALCN/UNC80 channelosome disorders, distal arthrogryposis, episodic ataxia, and paroxysmal dystonia were reported only in CLIFAHDD, whereas failure to thrive, central sleep apnea, and epilepsy were more frequent in biallelic NALCN/UNC80 loss-of-function disorders, IHPRF1 and IHPRF2 [PMID:40048676]. Heterozygous NALCN disease should also be considered in syndromic congenital ataxias [PMID:39914470], and MAGEL2-related disease may enter respiratory/metabolic evaluations when unexplained dyspnea is accompanied by metabolic acidosis and elevated lactate [PMID:29581464].

Attribution of congenital anomalies to the correct locus is critical. In a complex rearrangement, SHFM1/EEC manifestations from a 7q21.3 breakpoint were distinguished from Baker-Gordon-like neurodevelopmental and facial/ophthalmic features attributed to the SYT1 breakpoint [PMID:37916192]. Similarly, an IVD variant was deprioritized because the associated disorder is recessive and biochemical studies were normal, RXYLT1 was considered incompatible because its recessive phenotype was absent, and EFNA5 was not known to cause a Mendelian disorder, supporting SYT1 disruption as the best explanation [PMID:41438914]. Pubertal or endocrine findings may point elsewhere: in SOX11-related disease, constitutional delay of puberty was considered, but absent spontaneous progression, need for estrogen/GnRH-driven induction, and olfactory MRI abnormalities supported hypogonadotropic hypogonadism/Kallmann-like disease despite reported normal smell [PMID:42168980].

Competing etiologies in the diagnostic workup

Metabolic, mitochondrial, and endocrine leads. Metabolic acidosis with elevated lactate can reasonably prompt evaluation for fatty-acid β-oxidation or mitochondrial disease before a monogenic neurodevelopmental diagnosis is found [PMID:29581464], and skeletal-muscle complex IV deficiency may similarly direct testing toward mitochondrial disease even when mtDNA sequencing is negative [PMID:33665635]. Reported nondiagnostic workups have included lysosomal disorders such as neuronal ceroid lipofuscinosis, GM1/GM2 gangliosidosis, and mucopolysaccharidoses [PMID:32362337], disorders of glycosylation [PMID:32362337], mitochondrial disease [PMID:32362337], creatine/GAA disorders [PMID:32362337], purine/pyrimidine disorders [PMID:32362337], urinary organic acids and acylcarnitine profiling [PMID:41438914], and metabolic or vitamin/cofactor trials for treatable epileptic encephalopathies [PMID:37908909]. Nephrocalcinosis led to OCRL1 testing for Lowe/Dent disease with negative results [PMID:27811305], very-long-chain fatty acids were normal when chondrodysplasia punctata was considered [PMID:27811305], and SNRPN methylation was normal when Prader-Willi/Angelman-region disease was evaluated for neonatal hypotonia and feeding difficulty [PMID:27811305]. Endocrine testing may also enter the workup: endocrine function was normal in a child evaluated for tall stature [PMID:38321119], whereas absent pubertal progression with olfactory MRI abnormalities supported hypogonadotropic hypogonadism/Kallmann syndrome rather than constitutional delay in another monogenic neurodevelopmental case [PMID:42168980].

Structural, inflammatory, and acquired mimics. Normal brain MRI, including absence of hypothalamic or pituitary abnormality, helped argue against a suspected midline defect in one diagnostic evaluation [PMID:27811305], and normal brain MRI with absence of metabolic abnormalities was reported despite profound developmental impairment in SYT1-related disease [PMID:32362337]. Fever-associated seizures may raise concern for intracranial infection, but CSF examination excluded infection in most evaluated cases in one STXBP1 series [PMID:36440324]. Anti-MOG-associated encephalitis can coexist or confound the presentation when fever, CSF pleocytosis, serum MOG antibodies, and MRI white-matter changes are present; persistence of seizures despite immunotherapy prompted identification of an STXBP1 mutation in one patient [PMID:36440324]. Hypoglycemia should not be assumed causal when seizures persist after correction [PMID:37908909], and hypoxic-ischemic injury by history or MRI may be an incomplete explanation when a pathogenic epilepsy gene variant is also present [PMID:36440324]. Other acquired explanations considered or excluded in related cohorts included perinatal brain injury, traumatic brain injury, CNS infection [PMID:40548073], encephalitis, poisoning, and trauma [PMID:37171308].

Neuromuscular and respiratory differentials. Hypotonia, distal arthrogryposis, central apnea, and respiratory insufficiency can initially suggest congenital myasthenic syndrome, including RAPSN- or CHAT-related disease, but a neuromuscular/CMS panel without pathogenic variants argued against that diagnosis in one CLIFAHDD case [PMID:35055596]. The same workup excluded spinal muscular atrophy by SMN1 MLPA and familial dysautonomia by ELP1 sequencing [PMID:35055596]. Early apnea may also be mislabeled as breath-holding spells before recognition of an underlying channelopathy or neurodevelopmental syndrome [PMID:35055596]. In neonates with Crisponi/cold-induced sweating syndrome-like presentations, NALCN-related disease/Baker-Gordon–spectrum disease should remain an alternative diagnosis after negative testing for known CS/CISS genes [PMID:30859550].

Interpreting additional genetic findings. A true dual diagnosis is possible: Fragile X syndrome was an established co-diagnosis in one girl, but the phenotype was considered incompletely explained by Fragile X alone, leading to recognition of PPP2R5D-related disease [PMID:34946857]. By contrast, additional variants may be incidental or nonexplanatory, as with de novo SOS1, DCAF7, or CACNA1H variants excluded as causative because gene function and phenotype did not correlate [PMID:26576547], a PARK7 frameshift inherited by an unaffected sister rather than segregating with PPP2R5D-related disease [PMID:32743835], and a BPTF VUS considered unlikely to explain the presentation [PMID:35911839]. Other examples include a KDM5C variant not predicted damaging and clinically inconsistent with the expected disorder [PMID:27811305], a poorly conserved MAGEC1 variant not predicted damaging [PMID:27811305], and an XPR1 nonsense VUS considered less consistent with congenital disease although later primary familial brain calcification could not be excluded [PMID:29474920]. Secondary findings may explain only part of the presentation: SHFM1/EEC manifestations from a 7q21.3 breakpoint were distinguished from BAGOS-like neurodevelopmental, facial, and ophthalmic features attributed to SYT1 disruption [PMID:37916192], while recessive-gene or non-Mendelian breakpoint findings involving IVD, RXYLT1, or EFNA5 were not considered sufficient explanations for a Baker-Gordon–like phenotype [PMID:41438914].

Testing strategy and electroclinical interpretation. Broad molecular testing is often needed when routine laboratory, metabolic, EEG, ophthalmologic, and hearing evaluations do not identify an alternative cause [PMID:42750022], and long diagnostic journeys reflect phenotypic overlap among neurodevelopmental disorders [PMID:38283597]. Epilepsy-gene analyses should account for prior exclusions and data quality: an EIMFS cohort excluded known EIMFS genes before implicating SLC12A5 [PMID:27436767], while one individual was classified as intractable epilepsy rather than definite EIMFS because early ictal EEG records were unavailable [PMID:27436767]. EEG abnormalities also require clinical correlation; frequent epileptiform patterns in SYT1-related disease often lacked associated behavioral seizures [PMID:41332143], and suspected Lennox-Gastaut evolution in one STXBP1 case was not supported by typical EEG features [PMID:37908909]. When interpreting SYT1 or related findings, cohorts have excluded individuals with alternative pathogenic variants or variant classes not yet supported as causative, underscoring the need to separate the primary diagnosis from coincidental or insufficient genetic findings [PMID:41332143].

Management and Treatment

Epilepsy and epileptic spasms

Recognition and EEG confirmation. In Baker-Gordon/SYT1-associated disease, paroxysmal events should be classified carefully by semiology and EEG, because movement disorder, cyanotic/apneic episodes, and nonspecific EEG abnormalities can coexist with epilepsy; the SYT1 literature specifically calls for standardized, systematic EEG collection to clarify electrophysiology and symptom correlations [PMID:35101335]. When spells are uncertain, long-term video EEG is appropriate [PMID:29150892]. Serial EEG can guide relapse monitoring when epileptic spasms are a concern: in NEUROD2-related spasms, EEG abnormalities improved with treatment and reappeared before clinical relapse [PMID:36494631], and EEG normalization supported tapering levetiracetam after vigabatrin/prednisolone response in an STXBP1 case [PMID:37908909]. Recognition matters clinically: in SYNGAP1 encephalopathy, starting antiseizure medication after seizure recognition improved seizures, behavior, and sleep in one patient [PMID:30541864].

Antiseizure medication response is genotype- and seizure-type dependent. In Baker-Gordon/SYT1, carbamazepine produced a good response for nonfebrile tonic or tonic-clonic seizures in one reported individual [PMID:41438914], whereas a SYT1 case series found no clear benefit from carbamazepine, valproate, lamotrigine, levetiracetam, ethosuximide, or ketogenic diet when used for EEG abnormalities/severe impairment, with frequent sedation [PMID:30107533]. Other DEE reports show heterogeneous responses: SYNGAP1 epilepsy ranged from good control in one microdeletion case [PMID:23687080] to approximately 50% pharmacoresistance in a larger cohort with genotype-location effects [PMID:26989088], with seizure freedom reported on valproate, levetiracetam, steroids for spasms, topiramate, valproate/lamotrigine, and cannabidiol-containing polytherapy in individual patients [PMID:28576131, 30541864]. STXBP1 data suggest age- and seizure-type effects, with phenobarbital reducing focal seizure frequency and ACTH improving infantile spasms mainly in the first 2 years [PMID:35190816], while levetiracetam was associated with seizure freedom in several children in another cohort [PMID:36440324]. Favorable responses were also reported with valproate-based regimens in PACS2 [PMID:34405643], phenobarbital in an infant with PACS2 [PMID:38545008], potassium bromide/high-dose phenobarbital or ketogenic diet in some SLC12A5 cases [PMID:27436767], and drug-responsive epilepsy in KCNH5 p.Arg333His [PMID:36307226], whereas several genotypes had persistent or intractable seizures despite polytherapy [PMID:22429196, 29474920, 30455226, 39305655, 41233134].

Epileptic spasms require prompt syndrome-level treatment and follow-up. Reported spasm responses are mixed. In PURA-related disease, one individual responded to vigabatrin and another had spasm cessation with EEG normalization after ACTH following initial vigabatrin failure [PMID:29150892], while a PURA-deletion case had resolution of spasms and hypsarrhythmia with valproate [PMID:29619234]. In NEUROD2-related spasms, vigabatrin plus high-dose prednisolone stopped spasms within 24 hours, vigabatrin monotherapy failed during relapse, and adding prednisolone again stopped spasms [PMID:36494631]. In STXBP1, vigabatrin plus prednisolone or steroids improved seizure control in multiple cases [PMID:37908909], but one STXBP1 case remained refractory despite steroids, vigabatrin, valproate, topiramate, ketogenic diet, and VNS [PMID:37908909]. PPP3CA-related spasms were often treatment resistant: ACTH controlled spasms in 1 of 13, partially reduced them in 2 of 13, and failed in 10 of 13, while vigabatrin showed no seizure reduction in 13 patients [PMID:40548073]; however, one PPP3CA case had partial improvement with ACTH plus topiramate, clonazepam, and levetiracetam, followed by further relief after vigabatrin escalation, with persistent mild staring seizures [PMID:36158964]. Other single reports include seizure freedom after clonazepam plus prednisolone in GABBR2-related spasms [PMID:35414446], poor control despite valproate/levetiracetam and low-dose ACTH in PIGS [PMID:32612635], no meaningful response to topiramate or vigabatrin in TBL1XR1-associated West syndrome [PMID:37171308], and ACTH plus modified Atkins diet for pharmacoresistant HECW2-related West syndrome [PMID:40705299].

Dietary and acute therapies may be considered selectively. Ketogenic or modified Atkins therapy has variable evidence. In STXBP1, dietary therapy started at a median 4.5 months produced sustained seizure freedom in 25%, especially with later seizure onset [PMID:35998430], and ketogenic diet was effective for maintaining seizure freedom in comparative STXBP1 analysis [PMID:35190816]. Ketogenic diet controlled seizures in one SLC12A5 patient [PMID:27436767] and markedly reduced drug-resistant spasms in ATN1-related disease [PMID:38262122], but it did not control seizures in PPP3CA developmental epileptic encephalopathy [PMID:30455226, 40548073], PUM1 haploinsufficiency [PMID:29474920], or one PACS2 case until levetiracetam plus valproate was used [PMID:34405643]. For acute seizures in medically unstable presentations, midazolam stopped seizures quickly in a child admitted with hypoglycemia and seizures, although the patient later died of cardiovascular failure [PMID:29581464].

Pharmacoresistance and escalation. Clinicians should anticipate pharmacoresistance in a substantial subset and reassess seizure type, age, EEG pattern, and genotype before escalating, because apparent medication response may reflect seizure type and developmental timing rather than gene etiology alone [PMID:35190816]. Vagus nerve stimulation is a reasonable escalation option in selected drug-refractory DEE: one STXBP1 patient became seizure-free 20 days after VNS [PMID:36440324], another had psychomotor improvement despite already being seizure-free [PMID:36440324], and a PPP3CA patient became seizure-free for 6 months after VNS before relapse [PMID:40548073]. VNS may also fail; one STXBP1 patient continued to have seizures every 2 days despite extensive polytherapy, ketogenic diet, and VNS, with corpus callosotomy being considered [PMID:37908909]. Persistent uncontrolled epilepsy was also reported despite multiple antiseizure medications and/or diet in PUM1 [PMID:29474920], PPP3CA [PMID:30455226, 39305655], ATP6V1A [PMID:41233134], SPTAN1-associated West syndrome [PMID:22429196], and some SLC12A5 cases [PMID:27436767], supporting early referral to an epilepsy center when seizures or spasms remain active after appropriate first-line therapy.

Respiratory and airway support

In CLIFAHDD/Baker-Gordon syndrome, neonatal respiratory support may be needed; one NALCN/UNC80 cohort reported orotracheal intubation at birth for severe respiratory symptoms in 11.7% of individuals with CLIFAHDD [PMID:40048676]. Perinatal apnea in overlapping neurodevelopmental phenotypes has been managed with close respiratory monitoring and supportive care, usually in a NICU setting [PMID:29150892], and neonatal apnea episodes have been treated with supplemental oxygen [PMID:34946857]. Escalation to tracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation may be required for significant dyspnea [PMID:29581464], while recurrent infantile apnea has led to discharge with home oxygen in individual cases [PMID:29619234].

Sleep-related and episodic apnea/cyanosis should be managed with standard respiratory supports while the event type and triggers are clarified. In a CLIFAHDD case, oxygen and CPAP were used during respiratory episodes, and overnight CPAP was maintained or recommended to prevent nocturnal oxygen desaturation [PMID:35055596]. Central sleep apnea in infancy in SYT1-associated neurodevelopmental disorder was managed with supplemental oxygen and resolved by early childhood in reported patients [PMID:30107533]. Hyperventilation-triggered apneic/cyanotic episodes improved in one patient with iron, clonidine, and intermittent oxygen [PMID:30107533], and clonidine was reported to reduce sleep disorder and hyperventilation-induced cyanotic episodes in two older patients [PMID:30107533].

Airway clearance and chronic respiratory care may include inhalers or nebulized therapies, including budesonide and albuterol nebulizers [PMID:33665635]. For poor airway clearance, reported supportive measures include vest therapy, suctioning, and cough assist [PMID:33665635]. Because respiratory infections can be severe, close monitoring for infection is warranted; one reported patient died from pneumonia complicated by sepsis despite supportive management [PMID:41838201].

Obstructive or structural airway problems should be treated according to the anatomic contributor. Obstructive sleep apnea was reported before tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy in one individual [PMID:33665635]. Supraglottoplasty was used for swallowing difficulty/laryngomalacia in another reported patient [PMID:32362337]. These procedures should be considered when clinical examination, sleep evaluation, or airway assessment identifies an obstructive lesion amenable to intervention.

Medication-associated response signals should be distinguished from general supportive care. In one CLIFAHDD case with central apneas, pyridostigmine was associated with immediate dramatic improvement in apnea control [PMID:35055596]; after initiation, apnea attacks decreased, and urgent hospitalizations and respiratory resuscitation were no longer required [PMID:35055596]. Pyridostigmine was continued despite exclusion of congenital myasthenic syndrome because of its apparent positive effect on apnea frequency [PMID:35055596]. Separately, acetazolamide was reported as treatment for apnea in one individual with biallelic SLC12A5-related disease [PMID:27436767], but this should not be conflated with routine respiratory support or assumed to be established therapy for Baker-Gordon syndrome.

Nutritional and gastrointestinal support

Early feeding support should address neonatal feeding failure, inefficient oral intake, and failure to thrive. In one Baker-Gordon/SYT1 case, neonatal feeding failure was managed by transitioning from breastfeeding to formula with speech-therapist support [PMID:38283597], within multidisciplinary follow-up including nutrition, speech therapy, occupational therapy, and physiotherapy [PMID:38283597]. Failure to thrive may require early enteral access: one infant underwent percutaneous gastrostomy at 8 months for failure to thrive [PMID:27683084]. When oral structural anomalies directly impair intake, repair can improve feeding; after submucous cleft palate and bifid uvula repair, one child’s feeding and gross motor development improved substantially [PMID:27811305].

Dysphagia and swallow abnormalities require individualized management rather than automatic diet restriction. Severe swallowing or airway-related feeding problems prompted supraglottoplasty for swallowing/laryngomalacia in one SYT1-associated case [PMID:32362337]. Conversely, an inherited PURA case had videofluoroscopy-confirmed oropharyngeal dysmotility but did not require dietary modification [PMID:39131487], supporting management based on clinical aspiration risk, growth, and mealtime function rather than swallow-study abnormalities alone.

Escalation to enteral feeding is common when oral intake is unsafe or insufficient. In an age-related SYT1 cohort, nine individuals required surgical procedures to assist enteral feeding because of feeding difficulties [PMID:41332143], and another SYT1-associated patient required gastrostomy for severe reflux and feeding difficulty [PMID:32362337]. Other reported cohorts and cases describe nasogastric tube feeding for severe feeding problems in 4 individuals [PMID:30541864], discharge with a nasal feeding tube after persistent hypotonia and respiratory illness [PMID:29581464], gastrostomy for significant oral feeding difficulty due to hypotonia [PMID:29150892], gastrostomy at ages 5 and 9 years with another planned [PMID:33665635], gastrostomy for severe feeding difficulty [PMID:34212383], gastrostomy at age 8 years [PMID:35414446], and planned or existing gastrostomy for severe feeding or nutritional issues [PMID:26079862].

Reflux and constipation often need active treatment. Severe GERD may require fundoplication [PMID:29150892], and gastrostomy may be used when reflux coexists with major feeding difficulty [PMID:32362337]. Constipation should be anticipated and treated medically when needed: constipation required medical therapies in SYNGAP1-associated disease [PMID:26079862], and stool softeners or motility agents were recommended for constipation in PURA syndrome [PMID:29150892]. Iron supplementation worsened constipation in one child and was stopped [PMID:35055596]. Surgery for refractory constipation may not be effective; anal sphincter surgery for severe constipation was attempted in one patient but was unsuccessful [PMID:28576131].

Poor intake and food refusal may require acute metabolic support in addition to nutrition planning. One child with recurrent food refusal in childhood received glucose infusions during episodes of poor intake [PMID:28576131]. Glucose was also given immediately after admission for hypoglycemia with seizures in another child [PMID:29581464], underscoring the need to monitor hydration, weight, and blood glucose during prolonged inadequate intake.

Non-epileptic neurologic symptom management

Movement disorders and tone. In SYT1-associated Baker-Gordon syndrome, later-onset movement disorder, adaptive decline, and emotional wellbeing difficulties support longitudinal monitoring of motor function, adaptive skills, anxiety/obsessions, and mood [PMID:35101335]. Evidence for drug treatment remains case-based: one individual with the I368T SYT1 variant treated open-label with pramipexole for approximately 3 years had clinician- and parent-reported reductions in movement disorder severity, agitation, and self-injurious behavior, with increased responsiveness, but the authors cautioned that this single-patient observation cannot distinguish treatment response from natural history [PMID:30107533]. Spasticity in a D304G SYT1 patient was treated with baclofen, without a reported outcome [PMID:32362337]. In an ADCY5 dyskinesia cohort, clonazepam or clobazam produced mild functional gain in 6/7 patients, particularly for dyskinesia disrupting sleep [PMID:27061943]; clonazepam reduced axial hypotonia in 2/6 patients and improved falls from truncal extension dystonic spasms in one patient [PMID:27061943]. In that same cohort, levodopa, tetrabenazine, trihexyphenidyl, pregabalin, gabapentin, phenobarbitone, and baclofen were reported ineffective for movement symptoms [PMID:27061943], although one patient had temporary improvement in axial hypotonia and dyskinesia with clonazepam plus levodopa [PMID:27061943].

Parkinsonism and device therapy. Parkinsonism has not been established as a typical Baker-Gordon treatment target in the provided SYT1 reports, but levodopa-responsive early-onset parkinsonism was reported in PPP2R5D p.E200K, where all three patients responded to levodopa [PMID:32743835]. Carbidopa/levodopa intestinal gel controlled motor and nonmotor fluctuations in one PPP2R5D patient [PMID:32743835]. Dopamine agonist therapy in two PPP2R5D patients was limited by impulse-control disorders, including gambling, overeating, and hypersexuality [PMID:32743835]. Deep brain stimulation has mixed, diagnosis-specific evidence: bilateral pallidal DBS in one medication-refractory ADCY5 patient markedly reduced choreoathetosis/dyskinesia and modestly improved function, but did not improve axial dystonia/hypotonia [PMID:27061943], whereas DBS for PPP2R5D-associated parkinsonism had unclear benefit [PMID:32743835].

Sleep disturbance and episodic respiratory/autonomic symptoms. Sleep treatment is symptomatic. Melatonin was commonly tried for sleep disturbance in a SYNGAP1 cohort, with almost all affected individuals treated with or trialed on melatonin [PMID:26079862], and sleep improved with melatonin in 10 patients, clonidine in 5, and trazodone in 1 in another SYNGAP1 cohort [PMID:30541864]. In SYT1-associated disease, clonidine prescribed in the two oldest patients was reported to reduce sleep disorder and hyperventilation-induced cyanotic episodes [PMID:30107533]; in one I368T patient, iron, clonidine, and intermittent oxygen were associated with significant improvement in hyperventilation-triggered apneic/cyanotic episodes [PMID:30107533]. Insomnia was treated with clonidine in one POLR2A patient [PMID:33665635]. Clonazepam improved abnormal sleep patterns, irritability, and hypersensitivity to external stimuli in one FOXG1 patient [PMID:34964776].

Irritability, agitation, self-injury, and behavioral dysregulation. Behavioral management is also empirical. In a D304G SYT1 patient, agitation and self-injurious behavior were treated symptomatically with clonidine and fluoxetine, later requiring lamotrigine, risperidone, and valproic acid [PMID:32362337]. Severe behavioral abnormalities, mood swings, and agitation in a Baker-Gordon patient were treated with antipsychotic medications, but control was poor [PMID:41438914]. In the open-label SYT1 pramipexole case, agitation and self-injurious behavior decreased along with movement disorder severity, but causality was uncertain [PMID:30107533]. In a SYNGAP1 adult case, aggression and behavioral dysregulation required sequential antidepressants and behavior-damping medications beginning in adolescence [PMID:28576131].

Investigational mechanistic therapy. 4-aminopyridine is investigational only for SYT1-associated Baker-Gordon syndrome. In cultured neurons expressing approximately equal wild-type and mutant synaptotagmin-1, 4-aminopyridine dose-dependently enhanced evoked glutamate release and restored short-term plasticity [PMID:32362337]; at 5 μM, responses largely resembled wild-type neurons without drug [PMID:32362337]. The authors proposed centrally acting Kv-channel blockade as a rational potential pharmacotherapy based on this mechanistic rescue, but emphasized that clinical use would require additional considerations, especially convulsant effects at higher doses [PMID:32362337].

Habilitation and multispecialty follow-up

Coordinated habilitation should start early and be organized around a molecular diagnosis, which can improve management and enable diagnosis-specific follow-up [PMID:30859550]; surveillance and early intervention after diagnosis have been explicitly recommended in related neurodevelopmental syndromes [PMID:34212383]. Developmental care commonly includes early intervention for developmental delay [PMID:27683084], physiotherapy/physical therapy [PMID:27811305, 28576131, 29150892], occupational therapy [PMID:27811305, 38283597], speech-language therapy or speech stimulation [PMID:27811305, 37908909, 39131487, 38283597], hydrotherapy [PMID:37908909], and structured rehabilitation or neurological rehabilitation training [PMID:35911839, 36440324]. Educational planning should include special education when cognitive and language impairment limit learning [PMID:33963760], special-needs education for learning and behavioral difficulties [PMID:38321119], and classroom support or highly supported mainstream education when feasible [PMID:39131487]. Communication and adaptive function should be reassessed longitudinally: SYT1-associated/Baker-Gordon reports used child psychiatry evaluation and Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scales to characterize communication, socialization, and daily living skills [PMID:38321119], and later-onset movement disorder, relative adaptive decline, anxiety/obsessions, and mood difficulties in older SYT1-affected individuals support ongoing monitoring of motor function, adaptive skills, and emotional wellbeing [PMID:35101335].

Mobility and orthopedic care should be anticipatory, with periodic reassessment by physiotherapy, rehabilitation medicine, and orthopedics. Reported supports include ankle splints and/or Piedro boots to aid walking in most individuals in one syndromic neurodevelopmental cohort [PMID:26079862], bilateral ankle-foot orthoses for standing and walking in a child with balance impairment [PMID:35689525], and walker use for supported ambulation with ataxic gait [PMID:29474920]. Congenital or evolving lower-limb problems may require clubfoot redression and plaster casting [PMID:35055596], percutaneous Achilles tenotomies for talipes [PMID:27811305], Achilles tendon release for contractures or short Achilles tendons affecting gait [PMID:33665635], Pavlik harness treatment for congenital hip dysplasia [PMID:27811305], or hip dysplasia surgery such as osteotomy or open reduction [PMID:26079862]. Skeletal surveillance should include imaging for scoliosis, hip dysplasia, or other complications when clinically indicated [PMID:29150892], with vitamin D, calcium, and phosphate screening and periodic monitoring when antiseizure medications or other bone-risk factors are present [PMID:29150892]. Scoliosis or contracture surgery may be necessary in selected severe cases, although one older SYT1 patient had poor mobility after surgery for progressive contractures and scoliosis [PMID:30107533], and spinal fusion was reported for scoliosis in another SYT1-related case [PMID:32362337].

Feeding, swallowing, and functional supports require coordination among nutrition, speech-language, occupational, physiotherapy, and genetics teams, as described in a Baker-Gordon case followed by a multidisciplinary team [PMID:38283597]. Key functional issues include neonatal feeding failure responsive to speech-therapist-supported feeding strategies [PMID:38283597], severe feeding difficulty or reflux requiring gastrostomy or other enteral-feeding procedures [PMID:41332143, 32362337, 33665635, 27683084, 26079862, 34212383], swallowing difficulty/laryngomalacia treated with supraglottoplasty [PMID:32362337], and videofluoroscopy-confirmed oropharyngeal dysmotility that may not require dietary modification if clinically stable [PMID:39131487].

Sensory and congenital-anomaly surveillance should be phenotype-directed. Ophthalmology evaluation is relevant for poor vision or cortical visual impairment [PMID:27683084] and for nystagmus assessment and management [PMID:29150892]; strabismus repair was reported in developmental syndromes [PMID:26079862, 28576131], and corrective surgery for esotropia was reported in SYT1-related disease [PMID:32362337]. Audiology should be included when hearing loss is suspected or present [PMID:27683084], with hearing aids used for bilateral mixed hearing loss in one family [PMID:27811305]. Craniofacial anomalies may require cleft repair, including submucous cleft palate/bifid uvula correction with subsequent feeding and motor improvement [PMID:27811305] and cleft lip/palate repair in infancy [PMID:35689525]. Additional congenital-anomaly follow-up may include renal ultrasound and urine calcium:creatinine monitoring [PMID:27811305], urology surveillance for high-grade vesicoureteral reflux with recurrent urinary infections [PMID:41438914], cardiology evaluation for atrial septal defect [PMID:27683084], and repeat blood films for hematologic findings such as elliptocytosis [PMID:27811305].

Broader longitudinal surveillance and counseling should cover comorbid systems as they arise: endocrine follow-up for hypogonadotropic hypogonadism or pubertal delay [PMID:42168980], hepatic follow-up when liver dysfunction occurs [PMID:37171308], oncology/neurosurgery follow-up for rare tumors such as posterior fossa ependymoma and associated hydrocephalus [PMID:35689525], and close infection monitoring in medically fragile children given fatal pneumonia with sepsis in one multiple-congenital-anomaly neurodevelopmental syndrome case [PMID:41838201]. Neonatal jaundice may require phototherapy and follow-up [PMID:35911839, 41438914], and routine immunizations should be maintained when not contraindicated [PMID:38283597]. Genetic counseling should address whether the variant is de novo/simplex and the usually low recurrence risk while discussing reproductive options such as prenatal diagnosis [PMID:36494631, 38283597]; when a pathogenic variant is inherited from an affected parent, recurrence risk may be 50% [PMID:39131487]. In one Baker-Gordon report, predictive testing of asymptomatic siblings was not recommended because the authors considered it appropriate only for medically actionable conditions [PMID:38283597].
